# Chapter 19 — Cost: None

The second of Rust was a Tuesday and Corse read the entries out at the long table the way he had done on the second of the month for thirty-one years.

He got to 411,644 at about eight in the evening and Wick knew it was coming because he had watched the man's finger going down the sheet.

"*The Alder Second Setting*," said Corse. "*Common name: the seep. Class, Confluence.*" He paused, because the room had made a small noise at *Confluence* the way it had in Green, and he had learned to wait for it. "*Seat: the border of warm damp and white down. Effect: continuous slow release of water along the border. Does not cease.*"

Somebody said, "Water."

"*Feeds the adjoining terrain at approximately three quarters of a pace per season, unfed by Seed.*"

The noise the room made at that was different, and Wick, sitting eleven feet away with a bowl in front of him, did not know how to read it.

"*Cost*," said Corse.

And stopped.

He read it again to himself. Wick watched him do it. Then he put the sheet down on the table, flat, and looked up, and the Marrowfield Hall of Hunt went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when a man who never makes anything of anything has decided to make something of this.

"*Cost: none*," said Corse.

---

Nobody laughed.

That was the part Wick had not been ready for. He had spent four months being *ah*'d at, and he had built himself for mockery, and what happened instead was that about forty working hunters stopped talking and looked at a sheet of paper.

"Read the rest," said somebody.

"*The working is not performed. It is the border, and it runs whether the subject attends to it or not.*"

"That's not a working."

"That is what I said," said Corse. "That is *precisely* what I said, and I have been sat here for four minutes with it."

He picked the sheet back up.

"Right," he said, to the room. "I have read the entries out at this table on the second of the month since Y-seven-eighty-two, and I am going to say a thing I have never said, and I would like it understood by everybody here that I am not being funny.

"There is a Register in a building on Cross Street with four hundred and eleven thousand workings in it, and every single one of them has a cost against it, because a working *costs*. That is not a rule the Ledger made up. That is the arrangement. You spend and you get, and when you are spent you sit down for a week, and every man at this table has sat down for a week.

"And there is now an entry in that book with **none** written in the cost column, and it was entered by a boy of fifteen who holds Hearth on the boundary at nought point six weight."

Somebody down the table said, "Well, it's daft, then."

"It is either daft," said Corse, "or it is the most important line in that book, and I do not know which, and that is *exactly* why I mind."

---

He came over at about nine and sat down opposite, which he had never done, and put the sheet on the table between them.

Wick had spent an hour deciding what he would say and discarded all of it, because Corse's face was not doing what he had prepared for.

"Is it true?" said Corse.

"Yes."

"Every word."

"Sarrow wrote it. I answered eleven questions and he wrote it down and he made me say the cost four separate ways because he didn't believe it either." Wick pushed the bowl aside. "It's true and I can't prove it to you and that's the whole of the difficulty."

"Then explain it to me and use small words, because I am not a clever man and I have been doing this for thirty-one years and I have never heard of a thing that runs on nothing."

So Wick explained it, and it took about ten minutes, and he did it the way Sarrow had made him do it at the counter: not the effect, but the seat.

Two grounds. A border between them. The border does a thing that neither ground does. And he does not perform it, and cannot stop it, and it goes on at four in the morning while he is asleep in a room over a chandler's, and it has gone on since the twentieth of Dust.

Corse listened to the whole of it with his hands flat on the table.

"So it's not you doing it."

"No."

"It's your *ground* doing it."

"Yes."

"Then what is it costing your ground," said Corse, "because I have got fourteen acres of tenancy at home and I will tell you for nothing that there is not one thing on it that gives me water for free."

---

Wick sat in the Hall of Hunt on the second of Rust with a spoon in his hand and did not have an answer.

He had not asked that question. In eleven days of measuring a seep he had measured what it gave and had not once measured what it took, because it had not occurred to him that it might be taking anything, because *cost: none* had felt like a discovery instead of a hole.

"I don't know," he said.

"No," said Corse. "And that is not a crime and I am not accusing you of one." He turned the sheet round so it faced Wick. "Now I am going to tell you what I am going to do, and I am going to do it in front of you and not behind you, because that is how I was brought up and because you have answered every question I have asked without one word of clever.

"I am going to challenge your assessment."

---

The Hall had gone quiet again.

"You're entitled," said Wick.

"I am entitled, and I want you to hear *why*, and not the version you will get off this table by Thursday." Corse leaned forward. "It is not that you are on the boundary. There is not a man here who cares about that, whatever you have decided in your own head, and I will tell you plainly that four of them have said kind things about you when you were not in the room.

"It is that you are on the boundary at nought point six with a working in the Register that costs nothing.

"And there are two possibilities. Either that entry is wrong — and Sarrow does not enter wrong things, so I do not believe it — or **the assessment is wrong**, because a man who can put a thing in that book with none in the cost column is not a nought point six, whatever the frame said in Rust of last year.

"And if the assessment is wrong, then it is wrong in a way that has had you on Ember writs at Ember pay for a year," said Corse, "and I have watched you go down seam four two hundred times for four copper, and if it turns out this town has been underpaying a boy for a year because a hoop swung short, then I would like that on a form."

Wick sat very still.

"You think it's in my favour."

"I think it is *either way*," said Corse, "and I have been at this table thirty-one years and I have never seen a challenge lodged in somebody's favour and I have decided I do not care.

"A challenge is a public re-test. The frame comes out, an assessor works it, the whole town watches, and the number that comes out is the number. If it is higher than nought point six then you have been robbed and I will say so out loud in this room. If it is the same, then I will withdraw and I will buy you a drink and we will all find out that a border can give water for nothing, which is a thing I would like to know."

He stood up.

"I lodge it on Thursday," said Corse. "That is the fifth. It goes to the office and they set a date, and it is usually about a month, and you will get the paper. And I will say now, in front of a room, that I have got nothing against you, and that if I had wanted to hurt you I could have done it any Tuesday since Green and I have not."

---

Nettle found him in the yard behind the Hall about twenty minutes later, sitting on the water butt.

"I heard."

"He's right," said Wick.

"He's right about *what*, exactly, because I have had four versions of it walking across that yard and one of them had him spitting."

"He's right that a thing with no cost is either wrong or important." Wick had his elbows on his knees. "And he's right that I never asked what the seep is taking, and Nettle, I have not asked, I have not asked *once*, I have been sat there for a month writing down what it gives me like a man counting money he found in a coat."

"And the challenge?"

"The challenge is a re-test and a re-test measures weight." He looked up. "I'm nought point six. I've been nought point six since Dust of last year. I have got sixty-one breaths a night going into a patch that is now getting fed by a seep, and I have not put a frame on myself in eleven months because there has been no reason to and because it costs a shilling.

"He's going to lodge it, and in a month an assessor is going to put a hoop on me in front of this whole town, and it is going to say nought point six.

"And then everybody here finds out that the thing in the book is true, and that it doesn't help, and that I really am what the tally says."

Nettle sat down on the cobbles with her back against the butt.

"Then get heavier in a month," she said.

"You can't get heavier in a month. Nobody has ever got heavier in a month."

"No," Nettle agreed. "Nobody with a nine-foot room has ever got heavier in a month, because a nine-foot room saturates in a season and then it is what it is for the rest of your life, and every single thing anybody has ever told either of us about weight came out of somebody with a nine-foot room."

She looked up at him.

"You have got a border that feeds ground for nothing," she said, "and you have had it since Dust, and you have spent a month measuring how fast it makes the *patch* bigger.

"Has anybody in this entire country ever asked what a seep does to a man's *weight*?"
