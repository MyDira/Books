# Chapter 11 — The Bath-House End

*(Nettle)*

She picked the Hall, and she picked the bath-house end of it, and she thought about the choice for two days, which was longer than she had spent thinking about anything that was not a Hold since she was fourteen.

Not the workshop: that was Bri's ground and her mother's.
Not the room over the chandler's: that was hers and Wick's.
Not a street, because a street means walking, and walking means one of you can leave.

The bath-house end of the Marrowfield Hall of Hunt had four benches, a stove, and a rule that nobody talked business in it, and Nettle had sat on those benches since she was fourteen, and it was the only place in that town that belonged to what she *did* and not to who anybody's mother was.

She sent the note by Tarrow, who charged her a penny and was insulted when she offered him two.

---

Bri Alder came in on the twenty-third at four with her hair wet, which Nettle learned within a fortnight was simply how she always was, and sat down on the opposite bench and said:

"Right. Before you start. I didn't know."

"I know you didn't."

"Two years and she's said your name to me four times and every one of them was a fact, and I never asked, because it was obviously none of my—"

"I *know*," said Nettle. "Wick told me. I've had nineteen days. Sit down properly, you're going to fall off that."

Bri sat down properly.

They looked at each other.

She had Wick's eyes and Wick's colouring and none of his face, and she sat the way people sit when they have spent two years bent over a bench: forward, elbows on knees, weight on the balls of the feet. Nettle's mother sat exactly like that. Nettle had watched her do it for sixteen years.

"You've got her posture," she said.

"I have got a bad back off her posture," said Bri, "and she's told me about it twice and both times she said it while sat in it."

And Nettle, who had come here with about four questions and a considerable amount of armour, heard herself laugh.

---

They talked for an hour and eleven minutes and about half of it was nothing at all.

That was the part Nettle had not prepared for. She had built four openings and rehearsed the hard ones, and what actually happened was that Bri asked her about the strike, and asked it the way a person asks about work, and then asked six more questions, and the sixth one was **"how far can you get your hand from the thing before it stops going off?"** which nobody had ever asked her in her life.

"Two inches. Maybe three on a good night."

"Three's a lot. Three's not contact, that's *near* contact." Bri had gone somewhere behind the eyes. "What's it feel like at three?"

"Thin. Like it goes off but there's less of it there."

"Right, because you're spending the same and covering more air." She sat back. "Sorry. That's the job. Dor says I do it at people in the street."

"She does. She's always done it."

"Yeah," said Bri Alder. "She does."

---

The hard part arrived at about the fiftieth minute and Nettle put it there herself, because she had discovered that she could not make herself do it any earlier and would not forgive herself if she left.

"Does she mind?"

Bri did not ask what she meant.

She sat with it for a good while, and Nettle watched her decide to answer it properly instead of kindly, and thought: *that is where you got it, you got it off her, she has had you two years and you have caught it like a cold.*

"I'm going to tell you a thing about a pan," said Bri.

"All right."

"When she cures a chalk-hound Seed she goes out of the room first."

Nettle did not say anything.

"Not every Seed. Not river-drake, not crawler, not boar. **Chalk-hound.**" Bri's voice was very level. "She gets the pan on, and she gets it up to heat, and then she says something — it's always something, it's never nothing, it's *I've left the door* or *that's the wrong cloth* — and she goes out into the alley for about a minute, and she comes back in and does it perfectly.

"Every time. I have watched her do it, I reckon, forty times in two years."

"Did you ever ask?"

"Once. First year." Bri looked at her hands. "She said the fumes off a chalk-hound catch her throat, and I said *right*, and I never asked again, and I have known since about the fourth time that it isn't the fumes, because I have got the same throat as her by now and it doesn't catch mine."

The stove ticked.

"I didn't know why," said Bri. "For two years I didn't know why, and I've spent nineteen days knowing why, and I want you to understand that I'd have told you eleven months ago if I'd had the first idea."

---

Nettle sat on a bench in the bath-house end of the Marrowfield Hall of Hunt and was eleven years old.

She had been eleven for about four seconds and then she was sixteen again, and the thing she noticed on the way back was that she was not crying and was not going to, and that this was not toughness, it was that the information had gone somewhere too deep to get at from the outside.

Her mother had been curing a chalk-hound Seed at the bench in the workshop on Fell Street.
She had got her arm across the pan.
It had gone in about a quarter of an inch.
That was five years ago and it was an accident and nobody had ever once said otherwise, including Nettle, who at eleven had been mostly interested in whether she would have a scar.

And for five years, forty times a year, a woman with twenty-two years and a licence had put a chalk-hound pan on the heat and then walked out into an alley for a minute.

"She's never said," said Nettle.

"No."

"Not once. Not in five years. Not in Thaw, when she—" Nettle stopped.

"No," said Bri. "And she's not going to, and I think that's the bit you want to look at, because I've had nineteen days on it and it's the bit I'd want somebody to say to me."

"Go on, then."

"She's not not-saying it to punish you," said Bri Alder. "She's not-saying it because she thinks it's *hers*. She thinks she did it, and she thinks it's her business, and she has decided that telling you would be putting a weight on you that she dropped. And she'll go on deciding that until she's eighty, because she is the most stubborn person I have ever worked next to and I have got a brother."

---

Nettle walked home the long way, which took forty minutes and went nowhere near Fell Street, and she did not go up the road and she did not knock, and she was quite clear with herself about why.

Because she was not going to walk into that workshop and hand her mother a thing to be forgiven for.

That was the shape of every version she had run since Thaw, and she saw it now for what it was: she had wanted a *reason* — a wrong, a debt, something with edges — and Bri Alder had just handed her one, and it was so exactly the right size and shape that she had known within about four seconds that using it would be the cruellest thing she had ever done, including the sentence in Thaw.

*She thinks it's hers.*

Fine. Then it was hers.

Nettle got in at seven, and Wick was at the table with the ledger and two shillings counted out on it and a face like a boy who has decided something, and she said, before he could speak:

"I'm coming up the barrows with you on the fourth and I'm taking the writ and you're the hired hand, and we'll get your Seed, and I don't want to talk about this evening."

"All right."

"And I'll tell you one bit of it because it's yours and not mine." She hung her coat up. "Your sister asked me six questions about the strike and the sixth one was better than anything anybody in this town has asked me in six years, and she wasn't being clever, she was just *working*."

She sat down.

"And when I answered it, she went off somewhere behind her face for about four seconds," said Nettle, "and I've seen my mother do that exact thing about a thousand times, and it means the same thing when Dor does it, which is that she has thought of something and she is not going to say it until she is sure."

Wick looked up.

"What was the question?"

"How far can I get my hand from a thing before the strike stops going off." Nettle pulled the ledger over. "And the answer's three inches, and I have known that since I was fourteen, and I have never once written it down."
