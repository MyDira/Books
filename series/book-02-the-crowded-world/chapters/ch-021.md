# Chapter 21 — Through a Shell

Nettle worked it out on the seventeenth of Rust while watching him cast a crust on his own forearm for the four hundredth time, and she worked it out in about nine seconds, and then sat on it for two days to make sure it was not stupid.

"Do that again."

"I've done it twice."

"Do it slower and let me put my hand on it."

So he did it slower, and she put her hand flat on the shell while it set, and she felt a thing she had felt about nine thousand times in her life without once knowing what it was.

The set salt went *quiet*.

Not cold. Not hard, though it was that too. **Quiet** — in the way the bath-house went quiet when Sabbet came in, in the way a room goes when a thing that had been going stops going. Under her palm, a nutshell's thickness of Wick Alder's ground finished being two things and became one thing with an outside.

Nettle took her hand off and looked at it for a while.

"Say something," said Wick.

"Not yet," said Nettle, and went to bed, and did not sleep.

---

She got him up at six on the nineteenth and made him sit at the table and then walked about the room for four minutes before she said anything, and Wick, who had learned a great deal about her in a year, did not once ask her to hurry.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to say it in order and you're not to help.

"One. A working comes out at your Verge. That's the arrangement. Everybody's Verge is at the skin until Wold, and the working comes out there and stops, and the boundary is what shapes it. That's why a Hearth can't reach past his own hand and a Wold can.

"Two. I haven't got one."

"Yes."

"Three. So the strike doesn't come out at a boundary and stop. It comes out and goes until it's spent, in every direction at once, and three inches is not how far my *reach* goes — three inches is how far seven and a half weight gets before it runs out."

Wick sat forward.

"It's a *sphere*," he said.

"It is a sphere and I have been putting it against a flat thing my entire life." Nettle stopped walking. "Every time I have ever struck anything, half of it has gone into the air behind my hand and done nothing whatsoever, and I have known that since I was fourteen in the sense that I have never once thought about it."

---

"So you want a wall."

"I want a wall," said Nettle, "and I have got no boundary and I am never going to have one, and I have spent six years being told that's the whole of what's wrong with me.

"And you have got two hands and a working that puts a shell of set ground on whatever you touch."

The room over the chandler's was quiet.

"That's not a working," said Wick slowly. "That's — Nettle, the crust is *mine*. It comes off my border. It's my ground, on your hand."

"Yes."

"You'd be striking through my terrain."

"Yes," said Nettle, "and if you tell me that's the same as a graft I will hit you."

---

They argued about that for an hour and it was the only argument they had that year that neither of them enjoyed.

It was not the same as a graft. They got there in the end and it took an hour and Nettle did most of it.

A graft goes *in*. It sits in an arm for the rest of a life and does a thing when you open the door for it, and the whole of what is wrong with it — the whole of what she had refused at fourteen with a sentence she still believed — is that it is a working you did not make, living in you, doing the work for you.

A shell of set salt on the back of a hand is a **tool**.

"It's a glove," said Nettle, at about the fiftieth minute. "That's all it is. It's a glove somebody made for me. My mother has got four workings in her arm and I have got a glove, and if you cannot see the difference between those then you have not been listening to me for a year."

"I can see the difference."

"Then say it back."

"It's not in you," said Wick. "It comes off, and it cracks, and when it cracks it's gone and you're what you were. And you'd have to ask me every time."

"And I would *hate* asking," said Nettle. "I want that on the record. I have hated the whole idea for two days and I am doing it anyway, and the reason is that I have got seven and a half weight and I am sixteen and I have never once in my life been able to point it at anything."

---

They did it in the yard behind the Hall on the twentieth at six in the morning, with nobody about, on the horse trough with the iron band.

Wick cast the crust on the back of her right hand and up the wrist, thick, a full nutshell, and she waited for it to set and then flexed her fingers and cracked it across the knuckles the way it always cracked, and swore, and made him do it again with her hand flat.

Then she put her palm on the iron band and breathed out.

---

The noise was wrong.

That was the first thing either of them registered, and they both registered it at the same time and said so afterward. The strike is a crack like a hammer on rock, one, very short, and every person in Marrowfield who has ever heard it knows it.

This was lower, and it was not short.

The trough did not jump a quarter inch. The trough came off the cobbles by about four inches at the near end, went over sideways, and put forty gallons of water across the yard, and the iron band came off it entirely and hit the Hall wall eleven feet away and stuck in the mortar.

Nettle sat down very suddenly on the wet cobbles.

The shell on her hand was gone. Not cracked: gone, in flakes, all of it, and there was a silver handprint burned into a bit of iron on the other side of a yard.

"How much did that cost you," said Wick.

"One," said Nettle. "That was one. That was one of the three."

---

They measured it properly over the following four days, because they had a rule about that now, and the numbers went in Wick's ledger and in Nettle's own hand underneath.

**Without the shell:** three inches. One-third of her total per strike. Three strikes a day.
**With the shell:** they could not measure the reach, because it went into whatever she put her hand on and did not come out the back, and there was no back to measure.

What they could measure was the work.

An iron band a thumb thick, off a trough, into a wall, at eleven feet.

"That's a Wold's worth," said Wick, on the fourth day, and Nettle told him not to be stupid, and he said, "No — I've watched Vance do things. I've been paid four shilling a day to stand next to him for a year. I'm not saying you're a Wold. I'm saying that *particular thing*, on that trough, is a thing I have watched a high Wold do."

"Once a day."

"Once a day, and it takes both of us, and it costs you a third and it costs me a third of the wet." He put the charcoal down. "And there is no box on any form in the Sill for two people doing one working."

---

She said the last of it on the fourth evening, sitting on the wet cobbles with her back against the ruined trough, which the Hall had made them pay eleven shillings for.

"I want you to know what this is," she said, "because you're going to be nice about it and I'd rather do it myself.

"This is not me getting better. My Hold is exactly what it was. Two feet of glass, seven and four, no edge, and it will be that when I am eighty and there is not one thing anybody can do about it, and I have known that since I was fourteen and I have stopped arguing.

"What's happened is that I have found out what the hole is *for*, and it is for this, and it only works because I have not got a boundary, and every single person who has ever looked at my chit has read the hole as the thing that was wrong with me."

She picked a flake of salt off her sleeve.

"And I would like it noted," said Nettle, "that I did not find that out off a registrar, or an assessor, or four reassessments, or my mother, or a book.

"I found it out off a boy with no weight, in a room over a chandler's, because he said a thing about *edges* and I could not stop thinking about it for a month."
