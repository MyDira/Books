# Chapter 16 — Nine Hundred Years

They did the good news first and it took them a night and a half and it was the happiest Wick had been since Rust.

It went like this.

Pell's number, in the Alders' yard in Dust of Y-812, had been **nine hundred years** — the time to settle forty-two acres at eleven paces a season, assuming generously that it did not get harder, which it would.

That number had one assumption in it and the assumption was *Seeds*. Eleven paces a season was the rate at which a boy could kill things, cut Seeds out of them, and put them in the ground, and every single thing anybody had ever known about growing a Hold was built on it, because for eight hundred years there had been no other way.

The seep did not use Seeds.

"Three quarters of a pace a season off one border," said Wick, at the table, at two in the morning, with four candles going because Nettle had said *get another candle, I'm not doing this in the dark*. "And the border grows. And the growth grows the border."

"So do it."

He did it, twice, and got the same answer twice, and then made her do it independently, because they had a rule about that now.

**If a border feeds the ground either side of it, and the ground either side of it lengthens the border, then the thing does not add. It multiplies.**

Not fast. It was extremely slow: three quarters of a pace a season is nothing at all against two hundred and twenty thousand square paces. But it was slow *and* it compounded, and a compounding number does not care how slow it starts.

Nettle got to the end of the long division before he did and sat back and said an extremely rude word.

"Say it," said Wick.

"About a hundred and forty years," said Nettle. "If it holds. Which we do not know."

"A hundred and forty."

"And that's off *one* border, four inches long in Dust, at four shillings a Seed and eighteen shillings of my money."

Wick Alder put both hands flat on the table in a rented room over a chandler's on Cross Street and laughed until he had to stop and start again.

Nine hundred years had been a sentence. It had been the shape of the whole of him since a mule and a brass frame in his own yard, and he had carried it about the way you carry a bad tooth.

A hundred and forty years was still longer than a life. It was six times longer than a life. It was also **a number that had moved**, and it had moved because he had put two grounds next to each other on purpose and gone and looked at what happened, and it would move again.

"That's not the ceiling," he said. "That's one border. If I had forty—"

"Then it's a different number and neither of us can do that sum tonight," said Nettle, "and I want to write it down before you get clever, because I would like there to be a page in that ledger that just says the good thing."

So he wrote it down, and they went to bed at four, and for about eleven hours Wick Alder was as happy as he had ever been.

---

The other half arrived at three in the afternoon, in the street, on the way to the Hall, and he stopped walking so suddenly that Nettle went four paces past him.

"What?"

"How long is the seam?"

"Eleven thousand paces. You measured it in Seed."

"How long is it *now*?"

Nettle stopped.

They went back and got the ledger and did it on the table with the shutters open, and it took eleven minutes, and at the end of it neither of them said anything for a while.

**The seam's length, doubling about every three weeks since Dark:** 900 · 1,600 · 2,400 · 4,100 · 6,800 · 11,000 in Seed. He had not measured it since Green because he had been busy being pleased about a seep.

Wick went in and walked it that evening and gave up at **thirty-one thousand paces**, still a hand across, in ground he had called empty since he was fourteen.

---

"Right," said Nettle, when he came out. "Say it in order. Don't do the face. Order."

Wick sat on the floor by the stove with his back against the wall.

"One," he said. "A border feeds the ground either side of it and the ground lengthens the border, so the thing compounds. That's the good news and it's real and I checked it twice and so did you.

"Two. The seam is a border. That's all it is. Not terrain. It hasn't got an inside; I've had my hands on it, it's a hand across and it's a hand across at thirty-one thousand paces the same as it was at nine hundred.

"Three." He stopped.

"Go on."

"Three is that my seep is a border with two grounds either side of it, and it grows at three quarters of a pace a season, and it is four inches of a country the size of a parish.

"And the seam is a border with **salt on both sides of it**, and the salt is forty-three point nine acres, and it goes on doubling, and it has been doubling since Dark, and it will not stop, because there is nothing in that entire country for it to run out of."

---

He got up after a while and got the ledger and wrote it, because writing it was the only thing he had ever had.

> *Everything I have learned this year about how to make my ground live is a description of the thing eating it.*
> *It is not an invader. It is the same process. It is the SAME PROCESS, running on ground I do not own, and it is better at it than me by about a thousand times, because it is ALL border and no interior and I am all interior and no border.*
> *I have been calling it a countdown for a year and a half.*
> *It is not counting down. It is COMPOUNDING, and I have just this week worked out what that word costs.*

Nettle read it over his shoulder and did not say anything for a bit.

Then she said, "There's a fourth thing and you've not written it."

"I know."

"Write it."

"I don't want to."

"Wick," said Nettle. "I have sat here twice this year and made you say a thing you did not want to say, and both times it was the one that mattered, and I am going to keep doing it, and you may as well save us the argument."

So he wrote the fourth thing.

> *4. Every acre I settle gives it more border to grow along.*
> *The seam runs where two grounds meet. Right now it runs through salt, and salt is all there is, so it runs everywhere.*
> *If I plant forty patches to make three hundred and eighty paces of edge, I have not built three hundred and eighty paces of edge.*
> *I have built three hundred and eighty paces of NEW ROAD FOR IT.*
> *The method is the disease. I cannot use one without feeding the other. There is no version of getting stronger that does not also make it worse.*

---

Pell found them at nine that evening, sitting on the steps of the chandler's because neither of them had wanted to be in the room.

He did not sit down.

"You have got there, then," he said.

Wick looked up.

"You knew."

"I have suspected since the eleventh of Dust, and I have been sitting on a wall in this town for four months waiting for you to do it yourself, and I want you to understand exactly what that cost me and then I want you to forgive me for it, because I am going to do it again." Pell's voice had none of the cheerfulness in it. "If I had told you in Dust, you would have believed me. You would not have *measured* it. And in nine years you are going to need to be a person who measured it."

"That's a very convenient rule."

"It is an extremely convenient rule and I have examined it four times this year and it has held every time, and I am not asking you to like it." He put his satchel down on the step. "Now. May I say the thing you have both been carefully not saying to each other since three o'clock?"

Nettle said, "Go on."

"You are frightened, and it is the wrong fear," said Ossian Pell. "You have spent this afternoon frightened that the thing will reach the rim, which it will, in about eleven years at the current inward rate, and which will be extremely inconvenient and will not kill you.

"What you have actually found out today is much worse and much better, and neither of you has said it, so I shall.

"You have found out that your country is **not a container**. It is a *process*, and it has been running the entire time, and it has been running whether you were in there or not, and the thing you have been calling a seam is simply that process with nobody's hand on it.

"And you cannot stop a process. You can only ever be the fastest thing in it."

He picked the satchel back up.

"Which means the question is not *how do I stop the seam*," said Pell. "It never was. The question is one you are eleven years too young for and are going to have to answer anyway, and it is this.

"How fast can you grow?"
