# Chapter 18 — How Much Edge Has a Person Got

Bri asked Doreth on the eleventh and Doreth said no, and then said yes about four hours later, and the yes came with three conditions and a stare.

"He watches. He does not touch. He does not speak while my hands are on a man." She was setting the tools out with the spaces marked. "And if I put him out of that door he goes, and neither of you argues, and we do not discuss it after."

"That's fair," said Wick.

"It is not fair, it is *mine*," said Doreth Faring. "Sit on the stool by the coal chute, and if you are sick, be sick in the alley."

---

The man's name was Ollet and he was a drover and he was forty-one and he had come in about a hand.

Wick had never seen the thing Doreth did and had spent four months in Marrowfield imagining it as something like an operation, which is roughly right and misses the whole of it.

There was no cutting. That was the first surprise. She washed the arm and laid it out on a folded cloth on the bench with the sleeve rolled to the shoulder, and she talked to Ollet the entire time about a gate he was building, and Ollet talked back, and at no point did either of them say one word about arms.

The Seed came out of a pan. It was chalk-hound, and it had been curing since the ninth, and she had gone out into the alley for a minute before she put it on the heat, and Wick — sitting on a stool by the coal chute with his hands between his knees — had watched her do it and had said nothing at all.

She held it against the inside of Ollet's forearm, about two inches below the elbow, and stopped.

And moved it a quarter of an inch.

And stopped again.

Bri, at her shoulder, said nothing. Ollet went on talking about the gate. It went on for about ninety seconds, and Wick, who had come here to learn something and had expected it to be a technique, understood that he was watching a woman with twenty-two years *guess*, extremely carefully, in front of a man who thought she was chatting.

Then she put it in.

There was no sound. Ollet stopped mid-sentence and said "*oh*," in a mild voice, exactly the way Ock had said it in a lane in Dust, and Wick's stomach went over.

But nothing else happened. Ollet blinked, and flexed the hand, and said, "That's warm," and Doreth said, "It will be for a fortnight," and put a cloth over it, and that was the whole of it.

---

"How did you know where?" said Wick, afterward, in the alley, because he had been told he might ask afterward.

"I didn't," said Doreth Faring. "I guessed. I have told you that four times and you have not believed me once."

"You moved it a quarter inch."

"I moved it a quarter inch because two inches below the elbow is where the book says and it did not feel right, and I have got twenty-two years of it not feeling right, and that is the whole of my expertise and it is worth about eleven pound a year." She was cleaning her hands. "And in nine years if it goes bad he will come back and I will not know whether it was the quarter inch or the man."

"Bri says the seat's where a thing stops being one thing and starts being another."

"Bri says a great many things and about four in five of them are right, which is a better rate than mine." Doreth threw the cloth in the pail. "And neither of us can *see* it, and we have both known for years that seeing it is the whole of the job and that nobody has ever managed it."

Wick stood in the alley behind sixty-one Fell Street.

"I can see the Wild," he said.

---

It took Bri about four seconds and Doreth about ten, and Doreth's ten were, Bri said afterward, entirely spent on deciding whether to be angry.

"You can see the Wild," said Doreth.

"Since I was fourteen. Every Ember can. It's not a—" Wick stopped. "It's not a *skill*. It's the thing you get instead of a working. It's like — everybody kindled can see it, it's just there, it's like saying I can see the colour green."

"Mm." Doreth's face had not moved. "And can you see it on a *person*."

"Everybody's a post in a river. The Wild goes round you and drags behind you. Anybody can see that."

"Can you see it on an *arm*."

And Wick, who had been looking at people in Marrowfield for four months and had never once been asked to look at one part of one, said: "I don't know. I've never tried. Nobody's ever asked me to look at anything smaller than a town."

---

They tried it on Bri, because Bri put her arm on the bench before anybody had finished discussing whether they should.

Wick sat down on the stool and looked at her forearm for about a minute and a half in the light of four lamps and had to make himself stop being frightened first, and then he saw it, and it had been there the whole time.

"There's a — " He put his finger about an inch above her skin and moved it. "It's not the same all the way along."

"Say what you see. Don't say what you think it means."

"The drag changes." He moved his hand slowly from her wrist to her elbow. "Round the wrist it's tight and quick. Then about four inches up it *slackens*, and it's slack for a hand's width, and then it goes tight again. And there's another one — " he moved further "— just under the elbow, and that one's sharp. That one's like an edge."

The workshop was very quiet.

"Where under the elbow," said Doreth Faring.

"About there."

"How far below the joint."

Wick looked, and did the thing he does.

"Two and a quarter inches," he said.

Doreth Faring put both hands flat on the bench.

"I put Ollet's in at two and a quarter," she said.

---

They did nine arms in four days.

Doreth sent for people. She sent for four of her own old fittings and three of Bri's and two men who had never been fitted at all, and she paid them each fourpence and told them nothing, and Wick sat on the stool and said where the drag changed and Bri wrote it down in a hand better than his and Doreth said nothing whatsoever until the ninth.

The numbers came out like this, and Wick would spend eleven years on them.

**Every arm had the same three.** Wrist, mid-forearm slack, and the sharp one below the elbow. The distances differed by up to an inch and a half between people and were the same on both arms of the same person to within about a quarter inch.

**Old fittings sat on a change.** All seven of them. Doreth's four were within a quarter inch of a place where the drag changed; Bri's three were within an eighth, which Doreth looked at for a long moment and did not comment on.

**The one bad one sat between two.** A man called Sarrow — no relation, and he was insulted to be asked — had a graft in eleven years and going wrong, and it was a full inch and a half from any change Wick could find, sitting in the flat middle of a slack, and Doreth said, "Who did that one," and the man said a name, and Doreth said, "Yes," and left the room for a minute.

---

"So there it is," said Bri, on the fourth evening, on the crates in the alley. "How much edge has a person got. Three in a forearm."

"Three that I can see."

"Three that you can see with an Ember's sight, in four lamps, on a bench, at fifteen." She was turning her cup round. "What's a Field see? What's a Wold see? Vance has got fifty-two weight and eyes like a boiled egg and I would bet a month's money nobody has ever asked that man to look at an arm."

Wick sat on a crate in an alley off Fell Street.

"Nobody's ever asked anybody," he said. "That's the whole—" He put his hands over his face. "Bri, that's the *whole thing again*. It's the same shape as the borders. Everybody's had this the entire time. Every Ember in the Sill can see the Wild. Every Grafter in the Sill needs to know where a seat is. And there are four thousand people in this town and two thousand of them can see it, and it has never once occurred to anybody to put the two in the same room, because—"

"Because Grafters are Grafters and practitioners are practitioners," said Bri, "and a Grafter with nine feet of daub doesn't think of himself as a person who can *see* anything, and a hunter who can see the Wild has never in his life been in a workshop."

"And the Concord admits at fourteen and takes seven years."

"And the Ledger's got five boxes on a form."

They sat with it.

"I'm going to be sick," said Wick.

"You're not."

"I'm going to be sick because I have just worked out that this is worth more than everything I have ever done and I did it *by sitting on a stool*." He took his hands off his face. "I didn't make anything. I didn't kill anything. I sat on a stool for four days and said what I could see, and Doreth is going to fit better for twenty years because of it, and Bri, that is not a *thing*. There's no rank for that. There's no writ. There isn't a box on any form in the Sill for a boy who sat on a stool."

Bri Alder looked at her brother for a while.

"No," she said. "There isn't.

"And I'd like you to notice that you have just described the exact thing you have been angry about on my behalf since Green, and that you found it a great deal easier to see when it was mine."
