# Chapter 25 — Under Observation

He told them.

He stood on the flat stones on the north side of the market cross with a brass hoop still quivering beside him and told six hundred people the truth, in order, cost first, because that is how his grandmother said things and he had never found a better way.

It took four minutes.

A border between two grounds does a thing that neither ground does. He had one. It gave water, continuously, for nothing he could feel. It fed the ground beside it at three quarters of a pace a season. He had measured that eleven times and written it down and put it in the Register and the Register had entered *cost: none*.

And then, on the ninth of Rust, he had paid a shilling and found his acreage down half a point, and had spent four days telling himself it was the frame, and had paid two more shillings proving it was not.

"It isn't free," said Wick Alder, to a market square. "It never was. It's not making ground. It's *moving* it. The wet patch got bigger because the salt got smaller, and the salt is the thing I've got forty-three acres of, and I have been standing in the middle of it for a month cheering."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then a woman near the front, who he never identified, said: "So you're *spending* yourself."

"Yes."

"On purpose?"

"Not at first," said Wick. "On purpose since the fifteenth."

---

Registrar Moon let the square talk for about a minute and then put a stop to it, and then she did the thing she had to do, and she did it slowly and she did not enjoy it.

"Mr Alder. I am going to ask you four questions and I want the whole answer to each."

"Yes."

"Can you stop it?"

Wick had known that one was coming and had still not found a way to make it better.

"No."

"Say why."

"Because it isn't a working. I don't do it. It runs whether I'm asleep or awake, and I can't take it out, because you can't un-plant a terrain, and the border is between two things I put there myself." He heard his own voice go flat. "Sarrow wrote *the working is not performed* and I was proud of that line for a month."

"Second question. Is it accelerating?"

"Yes. The border gets longer as the patches grow, and the seep runs off the length. Half an acre in the first year. It'll be more than that in the second."

"Third. Do you know where it stops?"

"No."

"Do you know *whether* it stops?"

"No," said Wick. "I've got a theory that it stops when the salt runs out and that's nine years at forty borders and I've only got one, so it's — " he did it, because he could not help it — "it's about eighty years at the rate I'm going, and I don't know if the rate holds, and I'd rather say that than guess in front of six hundred people."

Ilsa Moon stood with her hand on the frame.

"That is the correct answer," she said, "and I am extremely sorry."

---

"Under the schedule," said Registrar Moon, to the square, "a Hold which loses recorded acreage without a recorded cause is entered **under observation**.

"It is not a punishment. It is four hundred years old and it exists because there is one other thing that makes a Hold get smaller, and it is not a border, and it is what happens in the eleven weeks before a bad rupture."

The market square went very quiet.

"A person under observation may not be hired to a hunt," said Moon. "May not enter a licensed Welling. May not hold or be named on a writ of any grade. May not be engaged, sponsored, or indentured. Is re-tested every ninety days at the office's expense, not his own, and the observation lifts when the cause is established and the fall has stopped."

She turned to Wick.

"I want the square to hear why," she said, "because half of them are already deciding this is spite and it is not.

"Two years ago a man called Hallen out of the Weft went into the Tallow lower gallery on a Wold writ with four other people. His acreage had come down a tenth and nobody had measured it because nobody measures. He ruptured at nine hundred feet in a party of five.

"Two of them came up.

"That is what the rule is for. It has been used forty-odd times in four hundred years and every single time the person it was used on was furious, and about one in nine of them was Hallen."

---

Corse said, "Registrar—"

"Yes, Mr Corse."

"He's not going bad. That's not — " Corse had his hat in his hands and had gone the colour of a brick. "Registrar, I lodged this. I lodged it to get the lad *paid*, and I have sat at that table on the second of the month for thirty-one years, and I would like it noted that I am not—"

"It is noted, and it is not your fault, and I would have found it in ninety days at his next tally," said Moon. "Mr Corse, if you had not lodged this, he would have gone down seam four next Tuesday with a falling acreage and nobody in this town would have known, and I would rather have him standing in this square being angry at me than at the bottom of a hole."

She began, carefully, to fold the frame.

"And I am going to say the other half of it, since I have said the first," said Registrar Ilsa Moon. "**I do not think he is going bad.** I have read the Register entry, I have read Sarrow's memorandum, and I have listened to a boy of fifteen explain a mechanism in four minutes with the cost first and the good news second, and people who are about to rupture do not do that.

"I think he is doing a thing nobody has done and that the schedule has no words for, and I have a status for the first and none for the second, and so I am obliged to use the one I have.

"That is not justice. That is a *filing system*, and I have been apologising for it for nineteen years."

---

The square emptied slowly and a great many people wanted to say something to him, and about four of them managed it, and Wick could not afterward remember what any of them said.

What he remembered was standing on the flat stones while two clerks put a brass frame back on a handcart, and Nettle not coming over, because she had seen his face and had known to wait.

And Coel of the Concord crossing the square and stopping about four feet away and saying, very quietly:

"An indentured apprentice is not entered under observation. The body carries it. That is the fourth thing an indenture buys and I did not tell you about it in Green because it would have been a threat."

"Is it one now?"

"No," said Under-Warden Coel. "It is an *offer*, and it is worse, and I want you to notice that I have waited until the worst possible day to make it, and that I am not proud of that, and that I am making it anyway because it is true."

He walked away.

---

They got him home at about six and he sat on the end of the bed for two hours.

The arithmetic was not complicated and he did it once.

**No writs.** Not Ember, not hired, not named on anybody else's. Nettle could work; he could not stand next to her while she did it.
**No Vance.** Four shillings a day, gone, and Vance would keep his word about it and that made it worse.
**No Welling.** Which meant no Seeds, which meant nothing to plant, which meant the only way he had ever had of doing anything.
**Every ninety days**, a hoop, at the office's expense, until a cause was established.
**And the cause was established.** He had established it himself, out loud, in front of six hundred people, and it had made no difference at all, because there was no box.

He had four pound one and eleven pence, sixteen Register entries, and a seep he could not switch off that was quietly spending the one number that had ever made him worth looking at.

"Say something," said Nettle, at about eight.

"Moon's right," said Wick.

"I know she is. That's not what I asked."

He sat with it.

"It's the same shape," he said. "It's the fourth time. Green of eight-twelve, Seed, Green again, and now this. Every single one of them closes, and every single one of them is a rule that's right, and every time the way out has been a thing nobody wrote down."

"So what's the thing nobody wrote down?"

"I don't know," said Wick Alder. "That's the difference. The other three, I had a week and a ledger and something to measure.

"I'm not allowed to measure anything. That's what *under observation* means. It means the one thing I have ever been able to do is now the thing I am forbidden to do, and they are going to come and put a hoop on me every ninety days until I can explain a thing that I can only explain by doing it."
