# Chapter 24 — The Fourth of Dark

The market cross at Marrowfield is a stone shaft on four steps with a lead roof over it, and on the fourth of Dark at two in the afternoon there were about six hundred people round it, which was more than had turned out for anything since the flood in Y-eight-eleven.

Wick had not expected six hundred.

He had expected forty out of the Hall and a dozen from Cross Street, and he stood at the edge of it at half past one with Nettle on one side and his sister on the other and looked at a market square with six hundred people in it and said, "Why?"

"Because it's free," said Bri, "and because there's a word going round that a boy has put a thing in the Register that costs nothing, and half this town thinks that's a lie and the other half wants it to be true."

"And the rest of them are here for the *arguing*," said Nettle. "Look at Sabbet. She's shut the coppers."

---

The office appointed **Registrar Ilsa Moon**, who had taken his Welling report at a counter on Cross Street in Dust of Y-eight-twelve and had underlined the last number in a knot profile and had never told him why.

She came out at ten to two with two clerks and the assessor's frame on a handcart — the real one, brass on a jointed stand, both hoops, and the third instrument in a felt-lined box — and she set it up on the flat stones on the north side of the cross and took eleven minutes over the feet, which Wick recognised, because Camber had taken eleven minutes over the feet on a gravel bar at a ford.

At two she stood up and read the notice out to six hundred people in a voice that carried to the back.

Challenger: T. Corse. Subject: W. Alder. Grounds: inconsistency between a recorded assessment and Register entry 411,644.

"This is a re-test," said Registrar Moon. "It is not a trial and there is nothing to win. I will read what the frame reads. If the challenger is satisfied he will say so and it ends. If he is not, he may say that too, and it still ends, because the frame does not care what anybody in this square thinks and neither do I."

Somebody laughed. Moon did not.

"Mr Corse. State it."

---

Corse came up the steps with his hat in his hands and said it plainly, in about ninety seconds, and he said it the same way he had said it to Wick across a table in a Hall on the second of Rust, which Wick had not been certain he would.

Not that the boy was a fraud. That the entry said *cost: none*, and a working that costs nothing is either an error or the most important line in the book, and a man carrying one is not a nought point six.

"And I'll say the rest of it in front of the town as I said it to him in front of the Hall," said Corse. "If the number comes up higher, then this parish has had him on Ember writs at Ember pay for a year and I want that on a form. And if it comes up the same, I withdraw, and I buy him a drink, and we have all learned a thing about borders."

He put his hat back on.

"Either way it wants doing in the open," he said, "and I'd not have it done any other way, and I'd like the boy's people to know I've not said one word behind him."

---

"Subject. Step in."

Wick stepped in.

Six hundred faces. A stone cross. Nettle at the front with her arms folded and Bri beside her and Doreth Faring beside Bri, which he had not known about and nearly undid him. Vance at the back on a horse. Coel of the Concord standing very still on the steps of the corn exchange with two men from the chapter house. Sarrow, who had shut the Register for the afternoon, which had not happened since Y-seven-ninety.

And Pell, sitting on the edge of the horse trough, with his satchel on his knees, writing.

"Weight first," said Moon, "because that is what is asked."

She reset the outer hoop with two fingers and let it go.

---

It went round and stopped.

Moon read it, and did not say the number. She reset it and let it go again, and read it, and reset it a third time, and Wick stood in a brass frame in a market square and watched a registrar do the thing Camber had done in his own yard, and understood that she was not surprised, she was being *careful*, because six hundred people were watching and a number said once is a rumour.

Four times.

"Nought point nine," said Registrar Ilsa Moon.

---

The square made a noise.

It was not a cheer and it was not an oh; it was about six hundred people saying something to the person next to them at the same time, and it went round the market like weather, and Corse — standing on the steps with his hat in his hands — shut his eyes for a second.

"Say it again," somebody shouted.

"Nought point nine," said Moon. "Recorded at nought point six on the fourth of Rust, Y-eight-twelve, by R. Camber of the Third Circuit, and I have the sheet and it is correctly taken and Camber is not in error. The subject has *gained* three tenths in thirteen months."

She let that sit for about four seconds.

"Which is not possible," she said, "and I would like the square to understand that I mean that professionally. A Hold saturates. A nine-foot room comes to its weight in a season and stays there for a life. I have taken four thousand assessments in this office and I have seen a weight move after the first year *eleven times*, and every one of those eleven was a person who planted a Seed, and this one has not planted a Seed since Gather."

Corse said, from the steps, in a voice that did not carry well: "Then it's the border."

"Then it is the border," Moon agreed, "and that is what the Register says, and the challenger's point is made, and I am going to enter it that way."

---

She turned to the crowd and did the thing that made Wick like her for the rest of his life.

"The challenge is **upheld**," said Registrar Moon. "The assessment of Rust Y-eight-twelve is superseded. The subject is at nought point nine, which is under the Hearth band and he remains on the boundary — I am not going to let anybody leave this square thinking otherwise — but he is not what the form says he is, and he has not been for some months, and this office did not know because this office does not re-test people who cannot afford a shilling.

"Mr Corse has done a public service and it has cost him a day's work and about eleven shillings in lodging, and I would like that in the day-book."

Corse went red to the ears and said, "That's not necessary," and Moon said, "It is entirely necessary and it is going in," and six hundred people laughed, and for about nine seconds the fourth of Dark was the best afternoon Wick Alder had ever had.

---

"Acreage," said Registrar Moon.

She reset the inner hoop.

She was doing it as a formality; he saw that, and understood it, and did not stop her, because there was no version of the afternoon in which anybody did not read the other hoop.

It swung out to the limit of its arm and quivered.

Moon read it.

Then she stopped.

She did not say anything at all. She crouched down and looked at the hoop from underneath, which cannot possibly have helped and which Pell had done in a farmyard in Dust of Y-eight-twelve, and then she stood up and reset it and let it go and read it again.

The square had begun to notice.

She did it four times, and then she sent a clerk to the handcart for the sheet, and she read the sheet, and she looked at the sheet for a considerable while with six hundred people watching her do it.

"Registrar?" said Corse.

"Forty-three point four," said Ilsa Moon.

Nobody in that square knew what it meant except about nine people, and every one of the nine was standing in a different place, and Wick watched the information reach them one at a time.

Sarrow, on the steps of the corn exchange, put a hand out and held on to the railing.

Coel of the Concord said something to the man beside him.

Doreth Faring's chin came up.

Nettle did not move at all.

And Ossian Pell, on the edge of the horse trough, stopped writing.

"The recorded figure of the fourth of Rust, Y-eight-twelve," said Registrar Moon, to a market square, in the flat voice of a woman reading out a distance, "is forty-three point nine."

She put the sheet down on the folding desk.

"Acreage does not go down," she said. "It has never gone down. There is no provision in the schedule for it to go down. There is not a box on this form to enter a fall and there is no form in this office that has one, and I have been in this service for nineteen years and I have never heard of it happening to anybody."

She looked at Wick.

"Mr Alder," said Registrar Ilsa Moon, "would you like to tell six hundred people what you have been doing, or would you like me to open a file?"
