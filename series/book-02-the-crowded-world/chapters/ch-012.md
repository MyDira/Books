# Chapter 12 — Her Writ

The writ came off the board on the morning of the fourth of High and it had Nettle's name written on the back of it in the Hall clerk's hand, and underneath, in the same hand, in the box marked *party*:

> *Alder, W. — hired person, 4s/day.*

"That's you," said Nettle.

"That's me."

"Say something."

"I'm not going to say anything, because anything I say will be about me, and it's your writ and I'd rather not." Wick shouldered the pack. "Also I've had it the other way round off Vance and he did it properly, so I know what properly looks like."

Nettle folded the writ into her coat.

"Nine shillings a day for me," she said. "Four for you. If you say one word about it on that road I'm leaving you at Fell Lane."

---

It is eleven miles to the barrows above Fell Lane and they did it in a morning with a cart as far as the Chalk turn and then on foot up the drove, and the drove in High is white dust to the ankle and the flies are indescribable.

They stopped at the top of the drove and ate, and Nettle went through the kit the way she went through kit, which was out loud and in order and twice.

"Salt."

"Nine pound."

"Line."

"Twenty-seven knots, and the good twine, and I've rewaxed it."

"Billhook. Spear. Wraps. Lamp, and don't light it up there, and I mean that." She counted it off on her fingers. "Bonesetter's kit. Vinegar and the grey stuff, because I promised your gran and I don't break promises to old women."

"You've never met my gran."

"I've had two letters off her." Nettle did not look up. "She writes to me. Since Dark. Did you not know that?"

Wick sat on the drove with a piece of bread in his hand.

"No," he said.

"Well," said Nettle, "now you do, and I'm not showing you them, and we're going up a hill."

---

There were five and they had come down off the barrows into the dry valley on the east side, which Nettle said was new and which she did not like.

They got up on the shoulder at four and lay in the turf and watched the valley go orange and then grey, and she talked him through it in a low voice with her chin on her fists, and it was the most professional thing Wick had ever watched anybody do.

Five hounds. Two of them big.
Wind coming up the valley, which was in their favour and would turn at about eight, which was not.
Three barrows in reach; the middle one was the highest and had a rabbit run through the base of it, which mattered because a rabbit run is a thing a chalk-hound will put its head into and get its shoulders stuck in for about a second and a half.
And a chalk cliff at the head of the valley, twenty feet, which was the reason she had picked the day: a thing that hunts by reading the drag off a Hold does not read *upward* well, and a person standing above one is quieter than a person standing beside one.

"That's not in the beast-book," said Wick.

"No."

"Where's it from?"

"Me. Forty-one writs." She scratched her nose. "I've never told anybody, because nobody's ever asked me a question about chalk-hounds, because I'm the girl with the strike who can't get past Ember, and the whole town has known what I am since I was fourteen and stopped listening."

Wick got the ledger out of his shirt.

"Say it again," he said. "Slower."

---

The plan was hers and it was good and it went wrong in the fourth minute for a reason neither of them had thought about, and the reason was Wick.

She went up on the middle barrow as bait, because that is what she is, and the pack came, and it came the way it had come in Dust of last year: out of the dead ground in a spread, not fast, converging on the loudest thing on the hill.

And Wick went out onto the open down behind them with a four-foot spear and his hands cased in salt and did not get eleven paces.

The rearmost hound turned round.

---

He stopped dead. It was about nine feet away and it had its head up and its front end swinging slowly through a quarter circle, and he could feel the ring of it going out over the turf and coming back, the way he had felt a tallow-crawler do it on a chalk floor a year ago.

*It's found a hole*, he thought, and then, in about a quarter of a second, with complete clarity: *no it hasn't. It's found a post.*

A year ago he had walked past six of these things at forty feet and not one of them had looked up, because there had been nothing to look at. Nought weight. No drag. A spear walking about on its own.

He was nought point six now.

That is not much. It is under a Thatcher girl and under a carter and under every kindled adult in the Loam. It is also **not nothing**, and a chalk-hound on open down does not care what your tally says; it cares that there is a small quiet post standing in the river where the water is going round something.

He had spent a year being pleased about nought point six.

It came at him.

---

He got the spear up and it went in and did nothing whatsoever, because the hide is loose and slides off a point, and he had known that for a year and had done it anyway because there was not time to do anything else.

Then it was on him and he was down in the turf with the whole weight of it and his forearms up, and the shells took the teeth — that was the entire reason he was not dead, and he had about a second to notice it — and it bit down on a nutshell of set salt across his left arm and the crust went with a crack like a plate under a thumb.

He put the wet on it.

He did not decide to. His right hand was flat against its shoulder and the hand did what it had done two hundred and seven times in a chalk seam under a licensed hill, and a patch of dry white hide the size of a dinner plate went soaked and warm.

The hound flinched.

*Wet hide does not slide.* He got his left forearm across its throat and his right hand into the soaked patch and it **held**, canvas-grip, exactly as it had a year ago on a hill four hundred yards from here, and he hauled the thing's head round and got a knee up and he could not reach the spear and he was not going to.

"NETTLE."

---

She was there in about four seconds and she came down off a twenty-foot chalk cliff to do it, and Wick did not see any of it and heard the last part.

The strike went off at three inches.

He felt it through the animal: the whole body jumped, and the sound came off the valley walls and back, and there was a quarter-second of flat blue-white light in which he saw, from underneath, a girl on one knee in the turf with her palm not quite touching a chalk-hound's spine and her face absolutely blank with concentration.

Then it was dead and extremely heavy and he had to be got out from under it.

---

They killed three more between them over the next two hours and the fifth got away down the valley, which Nettle wrote in the return, because she wrote what happened.

Then they sat on the middle barrow in the dark with a lamp they were now allowed to light, and Wick cut the Seeds out because his hands were steadier, and Nettle held the lamp and said nothing for about eleven minutes.

"Say it," said Wick.

"You've got heavier."

"Nought point six."

"Nought point six *is heavier than nought*." She turned the lamp down. "And I have taken you up here twice, and the first time not one of them looked at you, and tonight the back one turned round in four minutes."

"Yes."

"And in two years you'll be at one and a half and hold Hearth in full and be able to take a writ, and you will be *unable to do the thing you did in Dust of last year*, because the thing you did in Dust of last year was be nothing."

Wick sat on a barrow with a knuckle-sized chalky Seed in his palm and did not say anything for a while, because she had got there about nine seconds before he had and he was still catching up.

"Every practitioner on the Sill gets safer as they get heavier," he said at last.

"Yes."

"And I get *louder*."

"You get louder," said Nettle, "and everything on the Sill that hunts by drag has been ignoring you your whole life, and it is going to stop, one animal at a time, and nobody has ever had to think about that because nobody has ever come up from nought."

He wrote it on the ledger by lamplight, on the barrow, with the Seed in his other hand.

> *41. Coming up from nought is not the same as getting stronger. It is getting LOUDER.*
> *Every advantage I have had since Green Y-812 was being beneath notice.*
> *I am spending it. I did not know I was spending it.*
> *What else have I got that only works while I am small?*

---

They walked down the drove in the dark and got to the Chalk turn at about two, and slept in a hedge for three hours, and it was on the cart in the morning that he told her about the Concord.

"I'm not taking it."

"Right."

"You're not going to ask why."

"I'm going to ask why in about a minute, when you've stopped rehearsing it." Nettle had her hat over her face. "Go on then."

"Doreth said I'd break it inside a year. The teaching clause. She said you'd ask me how a boundary's made and I'd answer, because that's what I am."

"And?"

"And she's right." Wick watched the hedges go past. "That's the whole of it. It's not that the Concord's wrong and it's not that Coel's wrong, because he isn't, he's got four hundred failures and I've got a ledger. It's that I'd sign a paper knowing the exact clause I was going to break and the exact month I'd break it in.

"And I did that once already. I stood in a tree in Green and promised I'd go to Marrowfield before Gather and I went and I walked out of the office, and I have had a year of knowing what that feels like."

Nettle took the hat off her face.

"That's a better reason than I expected," she said.

"It's not a noble one."

"No. It's an *accounting* one, and it's yours, and I'd not have trusted a noble one off you." She put the hat back. "Right. So you've turned down Vance and you've turned down Coel, and both of them were correct, and you've got no list of four hundred failures and one chalk-hound Seed in your shirt."

"Yes."

"Then you'd better be right about the borders," said Nettle, "because there is nobody left in this country to be wrong at."

---

The Hall paid out at the counter on the sixth, and it paid them separately, because the return had two names on it and two rates.

Nettle first. Four chalk-hounds at two shillings the Seed, and the three-shilling standing bounty, and two days: **eighteen shillings**, counted out in silver, with the clerk saying *thank you, Faring* at the end of it the way he said it to everybody.

Then Wick.

"Alder. Hired person, two days. Eight shilling."

Eight shillings is not nothing. It was more than he had made in the five days before it. He signed the book with the mark he used because his signature was slow, and took the coins, and said thank you, and stood aside.

And the clerk, who was not being cruel and had never once been cruel to him, turned the page and said to the room in general, "Anyone else on the fourth?"

There was one more thing.

It came from behind him, from the long table, in a perfectly ordinary voice, and Wick did not know which of them said it and never found out.

"That's the salt lad, is it. On the *boundary*."

And somebody else said, "Aye," and that was all, and the conversation went on to something about a gate.

---

Nettle bought bread on the way back and did not mention it, and Wick did not mention it, and neither of them mentioned it that night or ever.

What he did instead, at about eleven, with the chalk-hound Seed on the table in front of him in a twist of oilcloth, was get the ledger out and turn to the loop he had drawn a circle round in Green.

He wrote underneath it:

> *I keep writing this down as though the loop is the problem.*
> *The loop is not the problem. The loop is a fact, like thin ground.*
> *Nobody has ever got out of thin ground by resenting it. Gran didn't. Dad didn't. They put a wall round nine paces of it and grew what would grow.*
> *STOP LOOKING AT THE LOOP. Plant the Seed.*
