# STYLE GUIDE — WILDHOLD
*Read before writing any chapter. This is the voice contract.*

## POV & TENSE
Third person limited, past tense. **Wick is the default POV.** Other POVs are allowed
but must be (a) a named section break, (b) no more than ~25% of a book, and (c) never
used to hide information the reader would resent losing.

## THE VOICE
Warm, dry, forward-moving. The narration is on the characters' side and finds them
funny. Sentences are mostly short. Paragraphs are mostly short. The prose gets out of
the way of the story and then, once or twice a chapter, does something lovely and
gets out of the way again.

**Model the rhythm on:** a friend telling you what happened, who is a very good
storyteller and slightly enjoys making you wait.

### Do
- Concrete nouns. Barley, brass, blood, boot-leather.
- Comedy from *character and system*, not from jokes. The Ledger's forms are funny
  because they are sincere.
- Let people be competent. Competence is the primary pleasure of this genre.
- Land every chapter on a turn — a decision, a reveal, a cost, or a door opening.
- Physical stakes rendered physically. Exhaustion, cold, the specific ache of a Hold
  running empty.

### Don't
- No purple. If a sentence has three adjectives, remove two.
- No grimdark. Bad things happen; the camera does not linger and the survivors make
  jokes.
- **No em-dash-heavy narration.** Use them, but sparingly and deliberately.
- No prophecy, no chosen one, no "he was destined."
- Never explain a system rule twice. Trust the reader; put the reminder in the *action*.
- No cliffhanger chapter endings more than one in four. Overuse is cheap and readers
  in this genre notice.

## ACTION SCENES — the house method
The genre lives or dies here. Every significant fight must have:
1. **A problem statement.** Not "he was stronger." *What specifically* about this
   opponent breaks Wick's usual answer?
2. **Terrain.** Both the real ground and both fighters' Holds. Fights are geography.
3. **A cost paid on-page.** Wild spent, ground lost, a technique burned out, a bone.
4. **An adaptation.** He tries the thing. The thing fails. He learns something *about
   the system* and tries a new thing. This is the beat readers came for.
5. **A clean resolution the reader can diagram.** If you cannot draw it, rewrite it.

Fights are 800–2,000 words. Longer only for book climaxes (up to 5,000, split across
chapters).

## THE NUMBERS
The reader is here for progression. Deliver it **explicitly and often**:
- Ledger assessments printed as small, clean blocks (see format below).
- Characters saying real numbers out loud. "Four acres. Weight nine." Not "he had grown
  much stronger."
- A visible ratchet at least once every three chapters: a Seed planted, a technique
  learned, a coherence grade improved, a rank taken.

### Ledger assessment block format
Use verbatim, indented as a blockquote:

> **LEDGER OF THE SILL — ASSESSMENT**
> Subject: Wick Alder, of Hollow Alder, the Loam
> Acreage: 41.2 · Weight: 0.0 · Coherence: ragged
> Terrain: none recorded
> Workings: none registered
> **Rank: Ember (unrankable)**
> Assessor: R. Camber, Registrar, Third Circuit

Use sparingly — 1–3 per book, at genuine milestones. They are punctuation, not wallpaper.

## CHAPTER SHAPE
2,200–3,200 words. Open in motion or in a question. Close on a turn.
No chapter is only travel, only exposition, or only feelings.
Every chapter must contain **at least one of**: progress, cost, revelation, or a
relationship changing state. Two is better.

## DIALOGUE
People interrupt, deflect, and talk past each other. Nobody explains the plot to
somebody who already knows it. Nettle is short and rude and precise. Pell talks in
complete paragraphs and answers a different question than the one asked. Wick asks
too many questions and does not notice he is doing it.

## RUNNING GAGS (maintain; add to `canon/threads.md` when you invent one)
- Pell's full title, which gets longer.
- Forms. Always the forms.
- Wick calculating acreage in barley-fields because it is the only unit he grew up with.
- Nobody can agree on the correct plural of "Wildborn."

## CONTINUITY DISCIPLINE
Every proper noun, technique, terrain, or rule you invent mid-chapter goes into
`canon/glossary.md` and `canon/continuity-log.md` **in the same work session**. No
exceptions. This is the single rule that makes 25 books possible.

# ⚠ THE TIC LEDGER — learned from the B01 c1–10 line edit
*Read this section before drafting. Every rule here was earned by a real audit finding.
These are the failure modes of THIS narrator, and at two million words each one becomes
the thing the series is known for.*

## HARD QUOTAS (per book, ~85k words)
| Tic | Found in B01 c1–10 | Quota |
|---|---|---|
| `, which was` + wry narrator gloss | 39 (one per 580 words) | **≤ 10** |
| Negation-as-definition ("It was not X. Not Y. Not Z.") | 254 not-forms | cut by a third; **one in three "It was not X" openings becomes a positive assertion** |
| Paragraphs opening "He"/"Wick" | 26% of all paragraphs | **≤ 18%**, and never 3 consecutive |
| Em-dashes | 1.24 / 1,000 words | keep under 3 |
| "for a while" / "for a long time" | 25 | ≤ 10 |
| Similes framed "the way ___" | 39 of 72 comparisons | **≤ half**; vary the frame |
| The number **eleven** | 30 uses, 4 different things measured at eleven feet | see below |

## THE DEFAULT-NUMBER RULE
This series' whole pleasure contract is *characters saying real numbers out loud*. A
number the author reaches for unconsciously destroys that, because it stops carrying
information. **Eleven** was the B01 default and has been redistributed.

- Before writing any specific figure, ask: *is this number doing work, or is it just
  sounding specific?* If the latter, pick a different one.
- **Protected numbers** (these mean something; do not spend them on scenery):
  · **eleven breaths / eleven paces** — Wick's rate and the size of the warm damp
  · **four** — one measurement is a guess, four is a number
  · **nine** — nine feet of room, nine knots on his mother's line, nine feet of grass
  · **forty-one point two** — the acreage
  · **thirteen feet** — Bri's Hold
- Rotate through the whole range for everything else. Odd, unlovely numbers read as
  real: seven, nineteen, twenty-six, forty-three.

## NO LIFESPAN PROLEPSIS  *(this one is close to a hard rule)*
The narrator may look forward. It may **not** say how long Wick lives.

- ✗ "…for the next sixty years." "…when he was three hundred years old." "…he would have
  them at eighty." "…it saved his life twice in the next three years."
- ✓ "…he never afterward stopped thinking about it." "Wick thought about that a great
  deal in later years." "…which Wick would later learn was what the man did instead of
  thinking out loud."

**The test:** does the clause report *how he changed*, or *how long he lasted*? The first
is voice. The second removes mortal jeopardy from every fight in twenty-five books, and
it violates the no-destiny rule by a friendlier mechanism.

## WICK'S COGNITION NEEDS MORE THAN ONE POSTURE
B01 c1–10 staged **every** act of thought identically: *he stops → sits down → turns it
over for a while → does the sum → files it → writes it down.* 44 instances.

Vary it. He must also think **while walking**, **while his hands are busy**, **out loud
to somebody**, and **badly, having to start again**. Keep the sitting-and-summing for
moments that deserve the ceremony.

Also: the construction *"X was what he had instead of Y"* is used three times to define
him ("counting… instead of a plan", "lists… instead of a temper", "writing things down…
instead of understanding them"). **Keep one per series.** It is a signature and signatures
should be rare.

## "RIGHT." — OWNERSHIP AND STAGING
"Right" as a full sentence is **Wick's**, and it is the sound of him accepting a fact and
starting work. Therefore:
- **Nobody else says it.** Not Tam, not Hob. (Both have been corrected in B01.)
- It must land **in conversation with a person** at least as often as into an empty room.
  Four of the first six were "Right," said into a space that does not answer. That is a
  cadence, not a tic.

## VERBAL FURNITURE — ASSIGNED, NOT SHARED
A deflection token used by three characters is a regionalism, not a voice.

| Token | Owner | Forbidden to |
|---|---|---|
| "Mm." | **Gran** | everyone else, Tam included |
| "Right." (full sentence) | **Wick** | everyone else |
| "Well." as a beat-opener | rotate; **never twice in a book by different characters** | — |
| "Course you did." (doubled repetition) | **Tam** | everyone else |
| "don't it," "'em," "Bout the size of" | **Hob** | — |

## THE FOUR ADULT VOICES — WRITTEN CONTRACTS
*(derived from what they actually became on the page; treat as canon)*

- **CAMBER** — no contractions. Subordinate clauses. Legal register. States the rule
  before the exception. **He contracts only when he is out of his depth** ("That's not,"
  / "I don't know"). This is his tell and it is load-bearing. Use it deliberately.
- **GRAN ALDER** — short declaratives. Numbers. A hard practical instruction to close.
  "Mm" as deflection. **Enumerates when angry** ("First… Second… Third thing"). Undercuts
  sentiment on sight.
- **HOB TUNNY** — dropped auxiliaries and elisions. Digresses into a second story before
  finishing the first. Delights in his own information. **Prices everything.**
- **TAM ALDER** — the weakest voice in B01 and he is in all 25 books. His one true thing
  is the **doubled repetition**: *"Course you did." A breath that was nearly a laugh.
  "Course you did."* Build only from that. Never give him Gran's "Mm" or Wick's "Right."
- **NETTLE** — short, rude, precise. Three words where three will do, one where they
  won't. Never explains herself. Says something devastatingly perceptive and denies it.
  Flatter, not louder, when angry.
- **PELL** — complete paragraphs. Enormous good cheer. Answers a *better* question than
  the one asked. Treats appalling danger and incorrect paperwork as equivalent problems
  and is slightly more upset by the paperwork. **Never lies.**
- **DORN VANCE** — flat, patient, a little tired. Explains a rule once. Does not raise his
  voice. "That's the rule" settles the matter, because to him it does.

**Attribution discipline (keep this exactly as it is):** every speech tag is `said`. No
muttered/snapped/retorted. No adverbs on speech verbs. B01 c1–10 ran 45 attributions,
all `said`. Do not change this.

## EXPOSITION — THE TWO TESTS
Roughly 9% of B01 c1–10 was system explanation with no character in the room, and two
passages failed. Before writing any block of world-explanation, apply both tests:

1. **Is there a person in it doing something?** The best exposition in B01 is the
   inventory of what an Ember is *for* — Sil drying the byre wall, Ma Dunnock and the
   ewes, Tam finding water. 418 words, all people, all doing. Keep every word of that
   kind and delete the rest.
2. **Does it pay off within two pages?** "Being an Ember meant you could see the Wild"
   works because he is about to look at a chamber full of it.

**Never restate a realisation.** B01 c5 stated the same insight four times. Once it has
landed, go straight on.

## OPENINGS — THE ELAPSED-TIME TRAP
B01 opened three of ten chapters on "N days have passed and here is what accumulated"
(c2, c6, c9). It is an efficient shape and it consolidates fast. **Max one per five
chapters.** Prefer opening on a character doing something; failing that, on an object, a
sound, a number, or a line of dialogue.

**Never open a chapter on a deed, an inventory, or a rate** with no person in it. (B01
c5's original opener was a tenancy agreement. It has been rewritten to open on Tam.)

## CHAPTER LENGTH
The 2,200-word floor is real. Four of B01's first ten fell under it, and the shortest
(1,941) was also the densest in narrator-gloss — **the gloss was doing structural work a
scene should have done.** If a chapter is running short, it is missing a beat, not a
sentence.

## WHAT IS WORKING — DO NOT "FIX" THESE
- Paragraph length. Mean 27 words, nothing over 150, no walls of text. This carries the
  series.
- All-`said` attribution.
- Closings. B01 c1–10 landed 10/10: every chapter turns on a decision, a number, a cost,
  or a door.
- Homely concrete similes: *the way she counted money*, *the temperature of a cow's
  flank*, *like old solder*, *a wasp's nest*.
- The "one measurement is a guess, four is a number" motif, which **varies** each time it
  recurs and reached its terminal form at the end of c10. Let motifs mutate; never restate
  one as explanation.
- Zero purple. Not one three-adjective stack in 22,000 words. Hold that line.
