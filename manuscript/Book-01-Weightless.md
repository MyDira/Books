# Chapter 1 — Thin Ground

The bread had not risen, which meant the cellar had gone thin again, which meant Wick was going to spend his morning in a hole in the ground with a candle and a piece of string.

He took the string off the nail by the door. It was a good string, waxed, with nine knots in it at even spaces, and it had belonged to his mother. He took the stub of candle too, and the tinder-box, and went down the cellar steps sideways because the steps were narrow and he had got tall in a way the house had not been built to expect.

At the bottom it was cold and smelled of turnips.

He set the candle on the floor in the middle of the room and lit it and lay down on his stomach next to it with his chin on his hands, which was undignified and was also the only way to see the flame properly from the side.

Then he waited.

The flame stood up straight. It did that for a while, the way flames did. Then, slowly, it leaned.

Wick paid out the string along the floor, knot by knot, from the base of the candle toward the direction of the lean. At the fourth knot the flame stopped leaning and stood up straight again. He marked the spot with a thumbnail scratch in the dirt and went back and did it three more times from three different corners, because his mother had taught him that one measurement was a guess and four was a number.

Four knots. Maybe four and a half at the north wall.

Last autumn it had been seven.

He sat up and blew the candle out and stayed there in the dark for a moment with his arms around his knees, doing sums.

The Wild pooled in low places the way water did, and a cellar was the lowest place on the farm, and if the lowest place on the farm was down to four knots then the farm was down to something under half a weight, and half a weight was not enough to proof bread in a morning, and it was not enough to keep a barley crop honest either.

It was, however, exactly enough to explain the last three harvests.

"Well?" his gran called down.

"Four," said Wick.

There was a silence from the top of the stairs of a particular quality. Gran Alder had a great many silences and Wick could tell most of them apart by now. This one meant *I already knew and I was hoping you would tell me otherwise.*

"Then we'll want the oven longer," she said. "Come up. You're letting the cold in."

---

The trouble with thin ground was that everybody knew about it and nobody could do anything about it, which meant that talking about it was the second most popular activity in Hollow Alder after complaining about the road.

The Wild was in everything. That was not a mystery or a secret; it was simply one of the things the world was made of, like water or weather or the fact that sheep were stupid. It was thicker in old forests and river bends and battlefields and thinner on high plains and salt flats, and Hollow Alder sat on a shelf of dry chalky nothing at the wrong end of the Loam, where it was thin.

Thin ground grew thin barley. Thin barley made thin rent. And thin ground, as the whole village had cause to know, made thin children.

Wick carried the loaves out to the oven behind the house and set them in and packed the mouth with turf, and while they baked he went and looked at the barley, because he had got into the habit of looking at the barley and had not yet found a way to stop.

It was the sixth day of Green. The barley should have been past his knee. It was not past his knee. It was a hand's width short of his knee and it had the pale washed-out colour of a thing that was going to survive the summer without ever exactly being alive.

His father was at the far end of the field with his hands in his pockets, looking at the same thing from a different angle, as though there were an angle from which it would look better.

"Morning," said Wick.

"Mm," said Tam Alder.

They stood there together for a while, companionably, saying nothing, which was how the Alder men had conducted almost all of their important conversations for four generations.

"Cellar's at four knots," Wick said eventually.

His father's jaw worked once. "Four."

"Four and a bit at the north wall."

"Right." Tam took his hands out of his pockets, looked at them, and put them back. "Well. Don't say it to your gran."

"I already said it to Gran."

"Course you did." A breath that was nearly a laugh. "Course you did."

---

Three days.

Nobody in the house had said it out loud in a week, which was how Wick knew that all three of them were counting.

You were kindled at fourteen. That was not a law exactly, but the Circuit came twice a year and there were only so many children, and a registrar with a folding desk and a schedule did not much care for the argument that your boy would be fourteen in a month. Fourteen was the number. Green and Rust were the months. The taper was lit, the frame was set up, the clerk wrote down what came out of you, and that was more or less your life.

What came out of you was a Hold.

Wick had heard it described perhaps two hundred times and he had never once heard anybody describe it in a way that made sense, which he had come to understand was not their fault. It was a place. It was a place inside you that was really a place, with ground and a sky, and when you shut your eyes and went in you were standing on it. Everyone got one. Almost everyone got the same one.

A room. That was the shape of it, everywhere, always, for as long as the Ledger had been writing things down: a room, four walls of some material that meant something to you, a floor, a ceiling, and one fire lit in the middle of it. Twelve feet across if you were from a village. Eighteen if your family ate well and you kindled somewhere rich.

In Hollow Alder they came out at nine feet and the walls were usually daub.

You could grow it. That was the whole point of everything, apparently. You went out and you killed things that were made of concentrated Wild, and inside them was a Seed with a piece of their country folded up in it, and you planted the Seed in your own country and it grew there, and then you could do what that country could do. Cold, if you planted cold. Fire, if you planted fire. Iron in your skin, if you were very lucky and could afford it.

And the more of it you had, the more you could hold, and the more you could hold, the more you could take, and so on, forever, up a ladder that Wick had only ever heard the bottom three rungs of, until at the top of it were people who had stopped needing the world.

That was the theory. In practice, in Hollow Alder, you got nine feet of daub and a fire, and you used it to keep the damp out of your feet in winter and to make the ewes lamb easier, and you did not think about ladders.

Bri had got eleven feet.

Eleven feet, brick, and the registrar had said *well now* out loud in front of everyone, and three months later she had walked to Marrowfield with a bag and a licence-application and had not been back except twice.

She had written for a while. Then she had written less. Then she had stopped, and nobody in the house said anything about that either.

---

He fetched water in the afternoon because it was his turn, and because the walk to the ford was the best part of the day and he was not above arranging his chores to get it.

The ford was where the road crossed the Wickwater, which had been named for the village and not for him, no matter what his cousins claimed. There was a stand of hollow alders on the near bank, six of them, old and split and standing up out of their own rot like a row of chimneys. The village had been named for those. Everything in Hollow Alder was named after the same three things, and Wick was one of them, and he had made his peace with it.

He filled both buckets and then stood in the shallows for a while with his boots off, because it was Green and the water was still cold enough to be interesting.

It was very cold, in fact.

He stood there a moment longer, frowning down at his own feet, white and wavering under the surface.

It was the sixth day of Green. There had been a week of proper sun. The Wickwater was a shallow brown farm river that ran over chalk and gravel and it should have been, by now, the temperature of weak tea.

Wick crouched and put his hand in up to the wrist and held it there and counted to thirty, which was as far as he got before his fingers started to ache.

Then he looked upstream, and downstream, and at the six hollow alders standing in their row, and had the specific unhelpful thought that he had been having about twice a week since the thaw and had not yet managed to say to anybody without sounding like an idiot.

*Something's wrong with the ford.*

He put his boots back on and picked up the buckets and went home.

---

Gran was on the bench outside the door in the last of the light, shelling something, which was what she did instead of sitting still.

Wick set the buckets down and sat on the step below her, which put his head at about the height of her knee, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"Three days," she said.

"I know."

"You'll get a room."

"I know."

"You'll get a *small* room," said Gran Alder, "because this ground is thin and the ground is most of it, and there's not a thing on this earth to be done about that, and I'll not have you lying awake for three nights thinking there is."

Wick said nothing.

"Your sister got eleven feet," Gran went on, "and your sister is not eleven-twelfths better than you, and you'll want to remember that in about four days when the whole village is being *kind* at you."

That was the part he had not thought about. The kindness. He thought about it now and found that she was entirely right and that he wished she had not mentioned it.

"They'll say it doesn't matter," she said.

"It does matter, though."

"It matters some." She cracked a pod. "It doesn't matter the way they'll be pretending it doesn't. Which is worse, because then you can't argue."

Wick pulled his knees up and looked out at the yard and the dark coming up out of the hedges, and at the sky, where the Rafters were starting to show. You could see them on clear nights: a faint fixed lattice up past the stars, too regular to be anything natural, going all the way over from one horizon to the other. Nobody in Hollow Alder had any idea what they were and the village position was that it was not their business.

"Gran."

"Mm."

"Does it matter where you're standing?"

She stopped shelling.

"When you kindle," Wick said. "Does it matter where you stand? If the ground's thin here and it's thicker somewhere else, and you stood on the somewhere else, would you get more?"

The silence that followed was one he had not heard before and could not classify.

"Camber sets his frame in the village square," Gran said at last, "because it's flat and it's got a wall for the wind."

"That's not the same as it not mattering."

"No," she agreed. "It's not."

"Has anyone tried?"

"How would I know? I never kindled." She started shelling again, harder. "Ask the registrar. He'll tell you it's not in the book, and then he'll go and look it up in the book, and then he'll come back looking hunted, because it won't be in there either way." She snorted. "That man's whole life is things not being in the book."

Wick sat with that.

The way he saw it, there were two possibilities. Either where you stood mattered, in which case standing on the thickest ground on the farm was worth doing. Or it did not matter, in which case standing on the thickest ground on the farm cost him nothing except a night's sleep.

He was, he had been told at various times by various people, an annoying boy. He asked too many questions and he did not notice he was doing it and he had the sort of face that made adults feel they were being examined. He had never particularly minded. What he minded was the other thing they said, the one they said fondly, which was that he didn't know when to leave a thing alone.

Because that one was true, and they meant it as a fault, and Wick had privately concluded a while ago that it was the only thing he had.

"Right," he said.

He got up.

Gran did not ask where he was going, which was one of the best things about her. He went inside and got the waxed string with the nine knots and the stub of candle and the tinder-box, and he took the lantern off the hook by the door and did not light it, and then he went out into the yard and stood for a moment working out where to start.

Forty acres of Alder tenancy. Call it sixty-six barley-fields, because barley-fields were the only unit anybody in Hollow Alder had ever measured anything in and Wick could not think in acres if he tried. Sixty-six squares. Four measurements each to make a number instead of a guess, and if he gave each one a fair chance to settle he could do maybe five squares an hour, which was thirteen hours, which was tonight and tomorrow night and most of the night after that.

He did not have three nights. He had three nights *and* the days in between, and the days had bread and water and the ewes in them.

He went and got the second candle anyway.

He started at the top of the home field, in the dark, lying down on his stomach in the wet grass with his chin on his hands, watching a flame lean, paying out a piece of waxed string knot by knot by knot.

The first square came out at three.

# Chapter 2 — Form Nine-C

By the third night the map had eaten the back of a feed ledger, both sides, and Wick had stopped being able to feel his elbows.

Sixty-six squares. Two hundred and sixty-four measurements. He had done them in the dark on his stomach in wet grass with a candle stub and his mother's string, and he had written the numbers down in a hand that got worse as the nights went on, and the pattern that came out of it was so boring that he had to check it twice.

The home field: three. The long field: three. The stony half-acre behind the byre that nobody had planted in eleven years: three, which at least was consistent. The orchard: three and a half, and Wick had felt a genuine flare of excitement about the orchard for almost a minute.

The whole tenancy sat between three knots and four, and the cellar was the best of it, and the cellar was a hole.

He had known that. He had measured it anyway, because a guess and a number were different things, and now he had a number, and the number said that there was no clever place on forty acres of Alder ground and there never had been.

Which left the ford.

The ford was not theirs. The ford was the village's, and the road's, and nobody's. It was also low, and wet, and shaded, and old, and every rule of thumb Wick had ever heard said that low wet shaded old ground held more.

He went down on the third night with one candle left and no expectations, and he lay on his stomach on the gravel bar with the six hollow alders standing over him like a row of chimneys, and he lit it.

The flame did not lean.

It lay down.

It lay flat along the top of the wick and pointed away from the water like something pinned, and it did not flicker and it did not go out, and Wick paid out the string one knot at a time with his heart going and reached the ninth knot and the end of the string and the flame was still flat.

He sat up.

Nine knots was as far as the string went. Nine knots was the string his mother had made for measuring a cellar, and he had never once in his life needed the last two.

He sat there on the cold gravel in the dark with a flat flame beside him, and thought, with perfect clarity, *that is not a farm number.*

Then he thought, *that is where I am going to stand.*

---

He told Gran because he told Gran things.

She listened all the way through without shelling anything, which she did not do often, and at the end of it she said, "Show me the string."

He showed her the string. She turned it over in her hands and counted the knots with her thumb, twice, the way she counted money.

"Nine," she said.

"It might be more. I ran out."

"Mm." She handed it back. "And you're going to tell Camber."

"I'm going to ask Camber."

"He'll say no."

"Probably."

"He'll say no," Gran said, "and then you'll ask him again, because you've never in your life let go of a thing on the first pull, and he'll say no again, and then you'll ask him a third time and he'll go and look in his book." She sat back. "Don't ask him a fourth time. There's a point where a man like that stops helping you and starts defending himself, and it's about a half-second after the third time."

Wick filed that away. It was, he would think later, some of the best advice anybody gave him that year.

"You're not going to tell me not to," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Gran Alder looked at him for a long moment in the dark, with the Rafters faint overhead and the smell of the oven still in the yard.

"Because there's thin ground and there's thin people," she said, "and I've watched this village turn one into the other for sixty years, and I've not once seen it happen to somebody who was still measuring things." She stood up, joints going off like green wood. "Go to bed. You look like a drowned cat."

---

The Circuit came into Hollow Alder at midmorning on the ninth of Green, which was two days late and, by the standards of the Third Circuit, punctual.

It came as a covered cart and a tired horse and one man. Registrar Rab Camber was thin and permanently damp and had the look of somebody who had been travelling for twenty-two years and had never once got the packing right. He set up in the square: a folding desk, a stool, a strongbox, an inkhorn, and the brass frame.

The frame was the thing everybody looked at. It was two hoops of brass on a jointed stand, one inside the other, and it did not do anything visible, and it cost more than every building on the square put together. It measured Holds. Nobody in Hollow Alder had the least idea how, and the general village theory was that it was mostly for show, and the general village theory was wrong.

There were eleven children that spring. Wick was the ninth on the list because the list was alphabetical by farm and the Alders farmed a strip called Whitts.

He spent the morning standing in a queue and being fourteen.

The kindling itself was quick, which was the part nobody warned you about. A child stood in the frame. Camber lit a taper and held it up. He said the words, which were not magic and were in fact a legal formula about eight sentences long establishing that the subject consented and was of age and was not currently indentured. The child breathed in.

And then something happened inside them that nobody outside could see, and their face did whatever their face did, and Camber watched the brass hoops and wrote three numbers down and said the rank out loud, and the family clapped, and it was over, and the next one stepped up.

Sil Thatcher's girl got nine feet. Daub.

The Rennick twins got nine and nine. Daub.

A boy called Ock, whose father had money and had fed him accordingly, got ten and a half feet of *brick*, and the square made an appreciative noise, and Ock's father went red with pleasure and bought a round at the Hollow that lasted until dark.

Wick watched the numbers go down in the ledger and did his sums and found that his sister's eleven feet was, in fact, extraordinary, and that nobody had ever told him that in so many words because everybody had assumed he knew.

Then it was the ninth name, and he stepped up, and did not get into the frame.

"Sir," he said. "I'd like to be kindled at the ford."

---

Registrar Camber had a particular expression for questions he had not been asked before. It involved a slow blink and a small movement of the jaw, as though he were putting something away in a drawer to look at later.

"The frame is set up," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"It is set up in the square, because the square is flat, and has a wall for the wind, and is the customary place."

"Yes, sir. Only the ground's thin here." Wick had rehearsed this for two days and it came out steadier than he had feared. "The whole village is thin. I measured it. It's three knots to four across our whole tenancy and four and a half in our cellar, and the ford lies the flame flat past nine, and I ran out of string."

Camber's pen stopped.

"You measured it," he said. "With a candle."

"And a string, sir. Four times each. From four corners." Wick produced the feed ledger, which was damp, and which had sixty-six squares on the back of it in bad handwriting. "One measurement's a guess. Four's a number."

Camber took the ledger. He did not look at it for very long. Then he looked at it for rather a long time.

"Who taught you to survey?"

"Nobody, sir. My mother taught me the string. It's for the cellar."

"It is not for the cellar." Camber turned the page over, and back. "It is a Sarrow's line, and it is in the Ledger's own field manual, section four, and I have not seen a civilian use one correctly in nine years." He handed the ledger back with the air of a man returning a knife he has decided not to think about. "It does not matter. I cannot kindle you at the ford."

"Why not?"

"Because it is not done."

"That's not the same as it not being allowed," said Wick.

The square went quiet in the way squares do.

Camber put his pen down.

Afterward, when Wick knew him better, he would understand what happened in that pause, and it would make him like the man very much. Registrar Rab Camber had spent twenty-two years on the Loam circuit. He had kindled something over four thousand children. He was Field rank, which was respectable and unremarkable, and he had long ago made his peace with being a small correct part of a very large correct machine. He was not a brave man and he was not an imaginative one.

But he was, absolutely and to his bones, a man who could not stand it when a thing was not in the book.

"No," he said slowly. "It is not the same."

He turned round on his stool and opened the strongbox and took out a volume about the size of a hymnal and four times the weight, bound in oxhide, with a brass corner missing.

The square watched a man look something up for eleven minutes.

Wick stood there the entire time with his hands at his sides. His father, at the edge of the crowd, had gone the colour of a man watching a cart go slowly over a cliff. Gran was sitting on the horse trough, entirely at ease, shelling something.

"Ah," said Camber.

He said it in a small voice.

"Form Nine-C," he said. "*Kindling at a place of the subject's nomination.*" He read on. His mouth moved. "Requires: the assent of the attending registrar. A witness of age. Written nomination of the site by the subject. And an additional fee of two copper, to defray the cost of transporting the frame."

He looked up. He seemed slightly stunned.

"I have never once been asked," he said, "and it has been in here the entire time."

"Two copper," said Gran Alder, from the horse trough, and stood up, and came across the square with the coin already in her hand.

---

They went down to the ford at the tail of the afternoon, because Camber had ten more children to kindle first and the machine did not stop for anybody, and by the time the cart came creaking down the road with the frame roped in the back the light was going amber and low.

Half the village came. Of course they did. Nothing had happened in Hollow Alder since the mill fire.

Camber set the frame up on the gravel bar with the two front feet in the water, which he did not like, and adjusted it three times, which took longer than the walk. The six hollow alders stood over the whole business like a row of disapproving chimneys. Somebody's dog got into the shallows and had to be shouted at.

"Written nomination," said Camber.

Wick had written it out that morning on a piece of the same feed ledger. *I, Wick Alder of Whitts strip in Hollow Alder, nominate the ford of the Wickwater as the site of my kindling.* It had taken him four tries and the first three were still in his pocket.

Camber read it, folded it, and put it in the box. "Witness of age."

"Mereth Alder," said Gran.

"Mark here." She marked. Camber stamped it, and blew on it, and the whole thing was suddenly, absurdly, a piece of paper that would exist in a strongbox in Sennet-on-Weft for two hundred years.

"Step into the frame."

Wick stepped into the frame. The water came over his boots, which he had known it would, and it was very cold, which he had also known.

He looked up. The brass hoops stood around him, one inside the other, at the height of his chest and his head. Beyond them was the crowd, and his father with his hands in his pockets, and Gran, and the alders, and the first faint scratch of the Rafters coming out in a sky that had not gone properly dark yet.

Camber struck a light and lit the taper.

"State your name for the Ledger."

"Wick Alder."

"Of?"

"Hollow Alder. The Loam."

"Are you of age, unindentured, and freely consenting?"

"I am."

The registrar held the taper up. The flame stood straight, and then it did not; it lay flat, pointing away from the water, exactly as it had for a boy on his stomach on the gravel three nights running.

Camber saw it. Wick watched him see it. Something went across the man's damp thin face that was not professional at all.

"Breathe in," said Registrar Camber, in a voice that had lost a little of its edges.

And out along the ford, upstream and down, the Wickwater went still.

Not slow. Not calm.

*Still* — the way a room goes still when someone stops talking. The reeds held their positions. A leaf that had been travelling stopped travelling.

Nobody in the crowd noticed. Later, when Wick asked, not one of them had noticed.

He breathed in.

# Chapter 3 — Forty-One Acres

It did not hurt.

That was the first surprise. Everybody who had ever described it to Wick had described it as a door opening, and doors did not hurt, but the way they said it had made him expect something with more violence in it.

What happened was that he breathed in, and the breath kept going.

It went past where breath went. It went down through his chest and out through the floor of himself and *kept going*, into a space that had been there the whole time behind a wall he had never known was a wall, and the wall was not broken so much as noticed, and once he had noticed it he could not stop noticing it, and the breath went through and he went with it.

And then he was standing somewhere else.

---

He knew, the way you know things in the first half-second, three facts about the place he was standing.

It was his.

It was flat.

And it was very, very quiet.

He opened his eyes, which he had not been aware of closing, and looked, and could not immediately make sense of what he was looking at, because there was nothing in it to hang the sense on.

Ground. Pale, hard, and level. It went away from him in every direction and did not stop. It was not sand and it was not chalk; it was flat and slightly ridged and it crunched a little under his boot, and when he crouched and touched it and put his fingers to his mouth, it was salt.

Above him there was a sky, and it was black, and there was nothing in it.

No stars. No Rafters. No moon, no cloud, no colour at the edges. Not the black of a night sky, which is full of things you cannot quite see. The black of a ceiling in a room with the light out.

And it was silent. Wick had grown up in a village of three hundred people and had never once in his life been anywhere silent. Even the deepest part of the night had wind in it, or a dog, or the house settling. Here there was nothing at all, and his own breathing sounded enormous and rude.

He turned all the way round.

"Right," he said, out loud, and his voice went out about six feet and stopped, as though the air were not interested in carrying it.

There were no walls.

That was the thing he had been not-thinking for several seconds now. A Hold was a room. Nine feet, twelve if you were lucky, four walls of daub or brick or whatever your soul had decided to be made of, a floor, a ceiling, and a fire.

He could not see any walls.

He could not see a fire either.

Wick stood in the middle of forty-one acres of salt in the dark and understood, quite calmly, that something had gone extremely wrong, and then he did the thing that he would do at every important moment of his life for the next sixty years, which was that instead of panicking he started walking to find out how bad it was.

He walked.

He counted paces, because counting was what he had instead of a plan.

At a hundred he stopped and looked back. He could not tell where he had come from. The ground behind was the same as the ground in front. There were no footprints in it. The salt closed up behind his boots like water.

At four hundred there was still nothing.

At a thousand he stopped, because a thousand paces was most of a mile and he had been walking inside his own chest for a while now, and because he had begun to be frightened in a way he did not have a word for.

It was not the size. The size was strange but the size was only strange.

It was that in all that distance he had not passed one single thing. Not a stone. Not a ridge. Not a dip where water had once stood. Nothing had ever happened here. Nowhere in any direction had anything ever happened at all, and it went on doing that, patiently, out past where he could walk.

"There's meant to be a fire," Wick said, to forty-one acres of nothing.

Nothing said anything back.

---

He came out of it standing in cold water with his hands shaking.

The light had gone amber to grey. The whole business had taken, he found out later, about four seconds.

"Steady," said Camber.

The registrar was not looking at him. He was looking at the frame, and his face was doing something that Wick, in his current condition, could not read at all.

The brass hoops were moving.

They were mounted on jointed brass arms and they were supposed to sit still and be looked at. Instead the inner hoop had swung out to the limit of its arm and stopped there, quivering slightly, pointing away downstream, and the outer hoop had gone all the way round to the pin and was pressed against it.

"That's not," said Camber.

He stopped. He took a breath. He put both hands flat on the desk that his assistant had not carried down here because he did not have an assistant, and which was therefore balanced on a rock.

"Step out of the frame, please. Slowly."

Wick stepped out of the frame. Water came out of his boots.

Camber reset the hoops with two fingers, delicately, the way you would set a broken bird. Then he stood back and looked at where they settled, and made a note, and reset them, and looked again, and made another note.

He did that eleven times.

The crowd, which had come down for a bit of an evening, had gone quiet in a way that crowds do when they realise they are watching something they will be describing for years.

"Weight," said Camber at last.

He said it to himself, not to anybody. He tapped the outer hoop. It swung to the pin and stayed.

"Weight is nil."

Somebody in the crowd said, "What?"

"Nil," said Camber, louder, in his official voice. "Zero. There is no Wild in it." He tapped the inner hoop. It swung out and quivered. "And the acreage is —"

He stopped again.

Wick would think about that pause for a long time. It was not a dramatic pause. It was the pause of a man doing arithmetic he had already done twice and did not want to do a third time.

"I do not have the notation," said Registrar Rab Camber.

---

It took him twenty minutes, in the last of the light, on a rock, with the whole village watching.

The form had boxes on it. The boxes said FEET. There were two of them, for length and breadth, because a Hold was a room and rooms had length and breadth, and there had been eight hundred years of forms with those two boxes on them and nobody had ever needed a third.

Camber crossed out FEET. He wrote ACRES above it in small careful letters, and then he wrote a note in the margin explaining that he had done so and initialled it, because you did not simply cross things out on a Ledger form, and then he wrote a second note explaining the first note.

Then he wrote the number.

"Forty-one point two," he said.

He said it flatly, into the quiet, and the quiet stayed quiet, because there was not a person on that gravel bar who had any idea what it meant.

"Is that a lot?" said Sil Thatcher.

"A room," said Camber, "of the usual sort. Nine feet by nine. Is one four-hundred-and-eightieth of an acre."

Sil Thatcher did some sums, visibly, and gave up.

"He has," said Camber, "roughly twenty thousand rooms."

He set the pen down.

"With nothing in any of them."

---

"There's meant to be a fire," said Wick.

"Yes."

"Everyone's got a fire."

"Yes," said Camber. "They have."

"So what's mine?"

The registrar looked at him for a while. He was a thin damp careful man at the end of a fourteen-hour day, and he had eleven more villages on his schedule, and he had just crossed out a word on a form for the first time in twenty-two years.

"I don't know," he said.

And Wick, who had been holding himself together on the assumption that somewhere in this there was a person who knew what was going on, felt the bottom go out of the evening.

---

The village was kind about it.

Gran had warned him and she had been exactly right, and it was so much worse than he had imagined that he almost laughed.

They said it didn't matter. They said plenty of good folk never amounted to a thing with their Holds and lived happy. They said his gran had never kindled at all and look at her. They said the ground was thin, everyone knew the ground was thin, it wasn't his fault. They said it would probably fill in. They said it in the specific gentle voices people use for a dog with a bad leg, and every one of them meant well, and by the time he got back up the road to the house Wick wanted to hit something and had nothing to hit.

His father did not say anything, which was the only mercy available in Hollow Alder that night, and put a hand on the back of his neck for a moment in the doorway, and went to see to the ewes.

Gran put food in front of him.

"Twenty thousand rooms," she said.

"With nothing in them."

"Mm." She sat down opposite. "And what do people do with a room?"

Wick did not answer. He was fourteen and he had just had the worst evening of his life and he was not in a mood to be taught anything.

"They put things in it," said Gran Alder, and got up, and left him alone with it, which was the kindest thing anyone did for him that year.

---

He could not sleep, which he had expected, and so at some point after the house had gone quiet he gave up on it and lay on his back in the dark with his hands folded on his chest and went in.

The salt was where he had left it.

He stood in the middle of it for a while. The black ceiling of a sky. The ground that had never had anything happen to it. His own breathing, too loud.

Twenty thousand rooms.

The thing was, and he turned it over carefully, because he had a suspicion that he was about to make a decision that would be difficult to unmake: everybody in the village had told him tonight that it did not matter, and every single one of them had been talking about a room. They were sorry for him because his room was empty. They were sorry for him the way you are sorry for a man whose house has burned.

But nobody had been sorry for him about *the size*, because nobody had thought about the size at all, including Wick, until this exact moment.

A room was nine feet across because that was what you got. You did not choose it. You could not buy a bigger one; Ock's father had money and had bought his son food and comfort and a good calm childhood and had got him ten and a half feet for it, an extra foot and a half, and the whole square had made a noise.

Wick had forty-one acres.

He did not know if that was worth anything. He did not know if it was worth *nothing*, and the honest evidence so far was that it was worth nothing, because the man with the brass frame and the book had written *unrankable* on the form and had not been able to look him in the eye.

But it was his, and it was enormous, and it was empty, and there was only one thing you could do with an empty place that size, which was find out how big it was.

He started walking.

He walked for what felt like an hour, and then for longer, out across the flat pale ground under a sky with nothing in it, counting paces until the count got too big to hold and then just walking, in a straight line, away from the middle, to see where it stopped.

It did not stop.

He was still walking when the cock went off in the yard and hauled him out of it, and he came up gasping with his heart going like a hammer, and lay in the grey dawn light in his own bed with salt on his tongue.

Then he got up and went to see to the barley.

# Chapter 4 — The Fire

The Circuit left at eight in the morning, which was three hours later than Camber had intended, because the horse had opinions.

Wick was there. He had not planned to be there. He had woken at four, failed to go back to sleep, done the ewes, done the water, done a great deal of standing in the yard, and then found himself walking down to the square with his hands in his pockets, on the grounds that the registrar was the only person in Hollow Alder who had not yet told him it didn't matter.

Camber was strapping the folding desk to the cart. He looked up, and did not look surprised.

"Alder."

"Sir."

The registrar went back to the strap. He pulled it, considered it, and unbuckled it and did it again, which Wick would later learn was what the man did instead of thinking out loud.

"You want to ask me something," Camber said.

"I want to ask you a lot of things."

"You have until this strap is done."

Wick had spent four hours assembling a list and it went out of his head entirely, and what came out instead was the thing underneath the list.

"What's the fire?"

Camber's hands stopped.

"That is," he said, "a considerably better question than the one I was braced for."

He finished the strap. Then he sat down on the tail of the cart, which put him at Wick's height, and rubbed his eyes with his thumb and forefinger like a man deciding to be late.

"When a Hold opens," he said, "the Wild that happens to be present at that moment goes in with it. All of it, at once. And it collects at the middle of the new ground and it condenses, and it lights, and it burns."

"For how long?"

"Your whole life."

"And the taper?"

"The taper is for the form." Camber almost smiled. "Eight hundred years of ceremony and the candle does nothing at all. Do not repeat that in front of a family."

Wick turned it over. "So the fire's what makes the weight."

"The fire *is* the weight. It burns and the ground round it soaks. A room of nine feet takes a season to saturate, and after that it is simply how a person is, and nobody thinks about it again as long as they live." He looked at his hands. "You draw to feed it. Breathe deep, feel out the Wild in the air, bring it in. Everyone's grandmother has a method and most of them work. It is the most boring discipline in the world and it is the entire foundation of the practice, and you may take that from a man who has watched four thousand children be told so and forget it inside a week."

"But I don't have a fire."

"No."

"So I can't draw."

"You can draw." Camber's voice had gone careful. "You can breathe in Wild all day. It will go into forty-one acres and spread out and it will register as nothing, because it *will be* nothing. A cup of water in a lake."

There it was.

Wick had spent a week being told it did not matter by people who did not know, and here at last was a man with a brass frame and twenty-two years and a book, telling him plainly that it did, and somehow it was easier to hear. At least it was true.

"Then what do I do?"

"I don't know," said Registrar Rab Camber, for the second time in a week, and he did not seem to enjoy it any better.

He got down off the cart.

"I will tell you what I *do* know, because you have been decent and because I am going to say something now that I would prefer you heard from me and not from a neighbour."

He took out the ledger, the real one, oxhide with the missing brass corner, and opened it to a page near the back where the columns were narrower.

"You are recorded as Ember, unrankable. That is not a rank. It is a note. It means the assessment was performed and produced a result the schedule cannot use." He turned the book so Wick could see, which he did not have to do. "The Circuit returns to the Loam in Rust. That is the ninth month. If you are assessed then and the result is the same, I am required to enter a second note, and the second note is not *unrankable*."

"What is it?"

"*Failed of Kindling.*" Camber closed the book. "It is a status, not a judgement, and I want that clearly understood. It exists for children whose Holds do not open at all, which happens, rarely, and it exists so that the Ledger does not spend a century assessing the same forty people. There is an appeal."

"Has an appeal ever worked?"

The registrar was quiet for slightly too long.

"Once," he said. "Y-604. The circumstances were exceptional and the man was already dead."

"Right."

"You would be barred from a hunting licence. From membership of a Hall. From apprenticeship in any licensed craft, which in practice is all of them." He put the book away. "You would be able to farm, marry, hold a tenancy, and live to seventy, and I would ask you to notice that a great many people would take that bargain gladly."

"Gran took it."

"Your grandmother," said Camber, "did not take anything. Your grandmother was never offered." He climbed up onto the cart and gathered the reins and looked down. "Six months. It is not very long and it is not nothing. Good morning, Alder."

The cart went up the road at a walk, and turned at the alders, and was gone.

---

The thing Wick had not understood until that week was what an Ember was actually *for*.

Nobody in Hollow Alder was anything above Hearth. Two of the older farmers were Hearth and both of them had got there by being kicked by things. Everybody else, the whole village, all three hundred, was Ember or was Gran's age and unkindled.

And Wick had grown up assuming that this meant nobody in Hollow Alder could do anything, and he had been comprehensively wrong, and he had needed to lose it to notice.

Sil Thatcher's girl had kindled nine days ago. Nine feet, daub. On the fourth day she had gone out to the byre with her mother and put her hand flat on the wall and stood there for a while with her eyes shut, and the damp had come out of the daub. Not dramatically. Over about half an hour the wall went from dark to pale and the smell went out of the corner, and that corner would now stay dry until autumn, and the byre would not rot, and the cow in it would not get the foot rot that had cost the Thatchers eleven shillings the year before last.

That was an Ember. That was what the whole village ran on.

Wick went round for a week and made a list, because making lists was what he had instead of a temper.

Ma Dunnock could tell you three days out whether a ewe was going to have trouble lambing, by standing near her, and she was right about four times in five, and in a bad spring that was the difference between a flock and a debt. Old Rennick could keep the well from going brackish. Two of the men could take the ache out of a back that had gone, not permanently, but enough to finish a harvest. Sil could dry a wall. His father could find water, which was so unremarkable in Hollow Alder that Wick had reached fourteen without registering it as a thing his father could do.

None of them could fight. None of them had ever seen a Wildborn. None of them had a working registered with the Ledger and most of them would have laughed at the idea. They had a fire in a nine-foot room and about one useful trick each, and the whole village stood up on those tricks, all day, every day, for eight hundred years.

And Wick had forty-one acres and could not dry a wall.

He tried. Obviously he tried. He went out to the byre when nobody was about and put his hand flat on the daub and shut his eyes and went in, and stood on the salt in the dark, and tried to want the wall to be dry.

Nothing happened. Nothing happened in a very particular way, too. It was not like straining at something heavy. It was like pulling on a rope that was not tied to anything.

He tried the well. He tried a sick ewe. He tried, once, late, feeling ridiculous, to make his own hands warm.

Nothing, nothing, and nothing.

---

At the end of that week he went out to the yard after dark and sat on the step and drew.

He did not know how. Gran had told him her method, which involved breathing out longer than you breathed in and thinking about the bottom of a well, and Gran had never kindled, and had learned it from a woman who had never kindled either, and Wick suspected the whole thing was folklore.

He did it anyway. He sat on the step in the dark with his hands on his knees and breathed out longer than he breathed in and thought about the bottom of a well.

And felt something.

That was the surprise. He felt it and nearly lost it and got it back: a thin cool thread of something coming in on the breath, in through the chest and down through the floor of himself and out onto the salt, where it went.

It really did just go. He followed it in and stood on the flat and could not find it. Forty-one acres had taken it and there was no telling where.

*A cup of water in a lake.*

He drew for an hour anyway. Then two. He sat on that step until the middle of the night with his back against the doorframe, breathing out longer than he breathed in, thinking about the bottom of a well, putting cup after cup into a lake.

At some point Gran came out, took one look, and went back inside, and came out again with a blanket, and put it over his shoulders without a word, and left.

---

He went in before he slept, the way he had done every night that week, and walked.

It was becoming a habit and he was aware that it might be a bad one. There was nothing out there. He had proved that four nights running. The salt went flat and pale and unmarked in every direction under a sky like the underside of a lid, and no matter how far he walked, nothing.

But he had begun to notice a thing about the walking, and tonight he tested it.

He walked a hundred paces out from the middle. Then he turned round and walked a hundred paces back, and he arrived at the middle. Exactly. Not near it. At it.

He tried it again at two hundred. Same.

Then he did the one that had been bothering him. He walked two hundred paces out, turned, and deliberately walked back on a wrong line, angled off by what he judged to be a good twenty degrees.

He arrived at the middle.

Wick stood there for a while.

He could not see the middle. There was nothing there to see; it was salt, like all the rest of it. But he could always find it, and he could not fail to find it, and it turned out that if you tried to walk past the middle of your own country, the country would not let you.

*So there's a middle*, he thought. *It's got a middle and everything's out from it. That's the only thing in the entire place and it isn't a thing, it's a direction.*

He filed it. He had no idea what use it was. He had begun to keep a list of things about his Hold that were true, and the list had two entries on it, and one of them was *no fire*, and now the other one was *there is a middle, and out.*

Two was better than one.

He came out and lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

Six months. Nine days gone.

"Right," he said, to the ceiling, and went to sleep.

# Chapter 5 — Rent

The Alders held forty acres at Whitts from the Chalk estate, on a nine-year tenancy with four years left to run, at a rent of eleven pounds six shillings payable at Gather.

Wick had known those words his whole life the way he knew the words of a hymn, which is to say completely and without ever once having thought about them. He learned what they meant on the fourteenth of Green, sitting at the table with a bowl in front of him, while his father and his grandmother had an argument so polite that it took him half of it to notice it was happening.

"Barley'll do six," said Tam.

"Barley'll do five and a half," said Gran.

"Six, if the back half comes on."

"The back half is not going to come on, Tam Alder, and you know it as well as I do, because you've been standing at the end of that field with your hands in your pockets every morning for three weeks like it's going to embarrass itself into growing."

Tam looked at his bowl.

"Five and a half," Gran said. "Wool's two. That's seven and a half against eleven six."

"There's the ewes."

"There's four ewes we can sell and still lamb, and that's another one pound twelve, and then we're at nine and a bit, and then we're short, and we've been short before." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you would read out a distance. "The question isn't whether we're short. The question is whether Nead takes it in arrears again."

"He took it in arrears last year."

"He took it in arrears last year," Gran agreed, "when there were two of us short in this village. This year there's five. When there's five, a man like Nead stops being obliging and starts being an example."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"What happens," said Wick, "if he doesn't take it in arrears?"

His father looked up. There was a particular expression Tam Alder got when one of his children asked him something he had been hoping to be asked in about four years' time.

"Then we'd be out at the Slack," he said. "And we'd go to Marrowfield and take wages."

"Both of you?"

"All three of us."

"All *four*," said Gran, "and don't you look at me like that. I'm seventy-one, not furniture."

---

It was the arithmetic that stayed with him.

Wick could not read well. He had never quite got the hang of it and had reached a comfortable arrangement with the world in which Gran read things out loud and he remembered them, which worked, and which was going to be a problem for him for years.

But numbers he could do. Numbers he could do in his head, fast, in his sleep, in barley-fields, which were the unit Hollow Alder measured everything in because a barley-field was what a man could reasonably work in a day and nobody there had ever needed another word for it. Sixty-six barley-fields at Whitts. Eleven pounds six against seven pounds ten.

Three pounds sixteen short.

He turned that number over for the rest of the day while he did the water and the ewes and the endless small work of a farm that was too big for two men and one of the men was fourteen.

Three pounds sixteen. Nine hundred and twelve pennies.

He had, in the entire world, a spear his father had made for boar, a tinder-box that had been his gran's, his mother's waxed string with nine knots in it, and forty-one acres of salt.

---

The carter came on the sixteenth.

Hob Tunny ran a cart between Marrowfield and the string of villages along the north Loam, once a week in good weather and whenever he felt like it otherwise, and he carried salt and iron and nails and news, and the news was free and worth roughly what it cost.

Half the village turned out for the cart. It was the only thing that happened.

Hob had a parcel for the Alders.

That in itself stopped the yard, because the Alders did not get parcels. Gran took it and turned it over twice. It was brown paper, string, and a wax blob, and it had *Alder, Whitts, Hollow Alder* on it in a good clear hand that Wick recognised across the yard from six feet away and did not say anything about.

Gran opened it at the table with the door shut.

Inside was a folded square of oilcloth, and inside that was eight shillings.

That was all. Eight shillings, in silver, in a fold of oilcloth, in brown paper.

No letter. No note. Not a word on the inside of the paper, not a mark on the oilcloth. Gran turned all three inside out and held the paper up to the window and there was nothing.

Tam sat down slowly.

"Well," said Gran Alder.

"That's near a third of it," said Tam.

"It is."

"Bri's got wages, then. She's got real wages, to be sending eight shillings."

"She has."

Wick watched his grandmother's hands, which were flat on the table on either side of the money and were not moving at all, and he thought: *Gran isn't glad.*

He was fourteen and he did not know very much, but he had spent his whole life at close range to that woman, and he knew the difference between the way she looked at good news and the way she was looking at eight shillings in a fold of oilcloth.

"She's all right, then," said Tam. "If she's sending money she's all right."

"Mm," said Gran.

"That's a good thing, Ma."

"It's eight shillings," said Gran Alder, "and it's the third time she's sent money and not sent a word, and a girl who's all right sends a word, because a word is free." She got up and put the silver in the crock on the shelf. "I'm not saying it's bad. I'm saying I'd like a word."

---

Wick went out and found Hob Tunny giving his horse a nosebag and asked him about Marrowfield.

Hob was delighted. Hob was always delighted. Nobody in the villages ever asked him anything except whether he had nails.

"Marrowfield," said Hob, "is four thousand people and a paved street, and I'll tell you the truth, boy, the street's the best of it."

"Is there a Hall?"

"Course there's a Hall. Every town's got a Hall."

"What's in it?"

Hob leaned on the cart and settled in with the air of a man who has been waiting eleven years for this.

"Board's what's in it," he said. "Great big board by the door with the writs pinned on it. Writ of Hunt, that is. Ledger posts 'em, Hall runs 'em. Something's eating sheep out past the Chalk, some farmer complains, Ledger writes a writ, writ goes on the board, and any licensed body can take it down and go and deal with it and come back and get paid."

"How much?"

"Depends what it is, don't it." Hob counted on his fingers. "Tallow-crawler, they'll give you four copper for the Seed and threepence for the head, and you'll want a strong stomach and no sense of smell. Chalk-hound's better. Chalk-hound's two shillings the Seed, and there's a bounty on top when they're bad in the barrows, and I've known that bounty go to five." He grinned. "Nine-in-a-coat, they say a proper Wold-grade beast'll fetch you eight pound for the Seed alone, but then you've got to be the sort of person who can kill a nine-in-a-coat, and I have met two of those in my life and I did not care for either of them."

Two shillings. Wick did the arithmetic without meaning to. Thirty-eight chalk-hounds and the rent was paid.

"Anyone can take a writ off the board?"

"Anyone *licensed*."

"How do you get a licence?"

"Ledger gives it you."

"How?"

"You go in the office," said Hob, "and you show 'em your tally, and if you're Hearth or better and you've not got a black mark, they write you a licence and take two shillings for it, and off you go."

"And if you're not Hearth?"

"Then you're not licensed."

"And if you hunt without one?"

Hob's grin went off him.

"Then you'd want to not," he said. "Ledger takes a very dim view. Fine's four silver first time and it goes up, and worse than the fine, they put a mark on your tally, and a mark on your tally follows you about like a smell." He shook his head. "And that's if the Ledger gets you first. If Vance gets you first it's a different afternoon."

"Who's Vance?"

"Dorn Vance." Hob said the name the way villagers say the name of weather. "Holds the licence for the whole Loam circuit out of Marrowfield. High Wold, they say. Grey eyes, and I mean *grey* — all through, like a boiled egg, on account of the chalk in him." He shivered theatrically and enjoyed himself. "He's not a bad man. That's the thing about him. He's not a bad man at all, he's just got no give in him anywhere, and if he catches you in a Welling without a paper he'll take your Seeds and walk you to the office himself and stand there while they write the mark."

---

That night Wick sat on the step and drew for two hours and put two hundred cups of water in a lake, and then he went in and walked, and came out no further forward, and lay in the dark and did the sum again.

He could not take a writ, because he was not licensed.
He could not be licensed, because he was not Hearth.
He could not be Hearth, because he had no fire and no weight and nothing in forty-one acres.
He could not get anything into forty-one acres, because the only thing that went into a Hold was a Seed.
And a Seed came out of a Wildborn, and killing one without a licence was four silver and a mark.

Round and round. He walked it four times and it closed every time, and it was so neat that he actually started to laugh, lying there in the dark, and had to stop because it would wake his father.

And then, somewhere in the fifth circuit, it came apart.

He sat up in bed.

He went through it again slowly, out loud, in a whisper, because he did not trust it.

The licence was to take a **writ**. The writ was a contract. You needed the licence to accept the Ledger's work and to **sell** what you brought back, because the Ledger paid out of a strongbox and the Ledger did not hand money to people who were not on a list.

Four copper for the Seed. Threepence for the head. *That was a sale.*

Nobody had said one single word about planting.

Wick sat in the dark with his heart going and turned it over for a long time looking for the hole in it, because a thing that looked this much like a door was usually a wall.

He did not find one. Not that night.

It was still illegal. He was clear about that. The hunting was illegal on its own, licence or no licence, writ or no writ, and it was four silver and a mark and Dorn Vance with the grey eyes.

But the reason he had been going round in circles was that he had been trying to get *money* out of the loop, and money went through the Ledger, and the Ledger had a list with his name not on it.

He did not need money out of the loop.

He needed a Seed. And a Seed did not need to be sold to anybody. A Seed needed to be cut out of a dead thing and carried home and put in the ground.

"Right," said Wick Alder, in the dark, to nobody.

Then he lay back down and stared at the ceiling for an hour, because there was one thing left in the sum and it was the largest thing in it, and he had been carefully not looking at it all night.

There were no Wildborn in Hollow Alder. There never had been. Thin ground did not grow them. The nearest Welling was Tallow, two hours the far side of Marrowfield, licensed and fenced and farmed and watched.

He had solved a problem he did not have.

Unless.

Wick lay very still and thought about a candle lying flat on a gravel bar, and a river that had stopped, and water in the sixth day of Green that was cold enough to make his fingers ache.

# Chapter 6 — The Ford

He gave it four days, because he wanted a number and not a guess.

The trouble was that he had run out of string. Nine knots was as far as his mother's line went and the ford had lain the flame flat at nine on the first night and every night since, and *past nine* was not a measurement. It was a shrug.

So on the eighteenth of Green, Wick sat down in the yard with a hank of twine off the byre door, a lump of beeswax, and a stick cut to the length of the gaps on his mother's string, and he made a new line with twenty-seven knots in it.

It took him most of an afternoon and he did it badly twice, and when it was done he sat looking at it with a feeling he could not name.

Twenty-seven knots. Three times as far as anyone in his family had ever needed to measure.

"That's optimistic," said Gran, going past with a basket.

"It's twine."

"It's twenty-seven knots of twine," said Gran Alder, "and I've been in this house seventy-one years and I've never seen anyone need more than six."

---

The ford lay the flame flat at twelve.

He got that on the first night with the new line, on his stomach on the gravel bar in the dark, and he checked it from four corners because one measurement was a guess, and it was twelve, twelve, eleven and a half, and twelve.

The cellar, the best ground on the tenancy, was four.

Wick sat back on his heels with the twine in his hands and looked at the six hollow alders standing over him in the dark, and did the only sum available, which was *three times*, and found it did not help at all, because he had no idea what the number meant. He knew what four knots meant. Four knots meant slow bread. He did not know a single fact about what twelve knots meant, because nobody in Hollow Alder had ever stood on twelve knots in their lives.

So he did the next thing, which was to find out where it stopped.

---

That took the rest of the week and it was, he would say afterwards, the most useful thing he ever did with a piece of string.

He worked it like a field. He started at the bridge post, which was the only fixed thing on the whole ford, and he walked the bank in ten-pace steps, and at every step he lay down and lit the candle and paid out the twine and wrote the number on the back of the feed ledger in his awful hand.

He did it in the dark because the flame was hard to read in daylight and because he did not want to be seen doing it, and both of those reasons were true and only one of them was the reason.

The numbers came out like this.

Up at the bridge post: five. Fifty paces down toward the alders: six. Then seven. Then seven. Then nine, in one step, which he did four times because he thought he had made a mistake.

Then twelve, at the alders.

Then, ten paces past the alders, going downstream: five.

He stood in the shallows at the ten-paces-past mark with the water going over his boots and read his own handwriting three times.

It was not a slope. That was the thing. Wild pooled like water; it came on gently and went off gently; the cellar was four and the yard was three and a half and the home field was three and you could not tell where one became the next. That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked, in every measurement he had ever taken, and he had now taken about four hundred.

This went five, six, seven, seven, nine, **twelve**, five.

It had an edge.

Wick stood in the cold water for a long time.

Then he waded out to the alders and put his hand flat on the nearest one, and it was the ordinary bark of an ordinary dead tree, and he took his hand off it and looked down.

The six hollow alders stood in a rough row along the gravel bar, up out of their own rot, split open down one side each the way old alders go, so that they were less like trees than like a row of standing chimneys. They had been there before the village and the village was named for them.

The third one along had water in the bottom of it.

That was not strange. They were hollow and it rained. What was strange, and what Wick noticed because he had been on his hands and knees on that gravel bar for five nights and had got to know it better than he knew his own yard, was that the water in the bottom of the third alder was *lower than the river*.

The river was four feet away. The gravel between was soaked. Any hole within four feet of the Wickwater filled to the level of the Wickwater; that was not magic, that was digging a well, and every child in the Loam knew it.

The water in the third alder stood a good hand below.

Which meant it was going somewhere.

---

He went home. He did not go home because he was frightened, although he was; he went home because it was the middle of the night and he had no lamp and no rope and no sense, and he had a very clear idea that whatever this was, it deserved a second visit in daylight with equipment.

He got neither, because at breakfast his father said, mildly, "Ewe's down in the top field," and the day went.

He got back to the ford on the twenty-fourth of Green with a lantern, forty feet of the good rope, and his father's boar spear, and told Gran he was going to look at the ford, which was true.

Gran had looked at the rope.

"Mm," she had said.

That was all. He had thought about that "mm" quite a lot since.

---

In daylight the hollow alder was less impressive and more obviously wrong.

He got up into it, which was awkward, and knelt in the split with the lantern down between his knees, and looked at what was at the bottom.

The bottom of the tree was not the bottom of the tree. The rot had gone through and taken the base out, and under it the gravel had gone too, and what was there was a hole about the width of his shoulders going down at an angle into the bank.

He could see perhaps eight feet of it. It was wet. It went on.

Air came up out of it.

That was the part he had not expected and the part that made his arms go strange. Air came up out of it, steadily, not in gusts, and it was cold, and it did not smell like a hole in a riverbank. It did not smell of rot or mud or river. It smelled of nothing at all, in a thick way, the way the cellar smelled of turnips and this smelled of *no turnips*, aggressively, as though the air had been washed.

Wick lit the candle and held it at the mouth.

The flame lay flat and pointed straight up out of the hole, and stayed there, and did not so much as tremble.

He put the candle out. He sat back in the split of the tree with his boots braced and looked at the sky through the alder branches for a while and let his heart come down.

Then he took out the twine and lowered the candle in on it, lit, which took four tries.

At the first knot the flame lay flat. At the second knot it lay flat. Somewhere between the third and the fourth it went out, and when he hauled it up the wick was cold and the wax was cold and there was no smoke on it at all.

It had not blown out.

---

Wick Alder sat in a dead tree above a hole in the world for the better part of an hour and did the thinking that decided the rest of his life, and to his credit he did it honestly, which is rarer than it sounds.

He knew what it was. Everybody knew what it was, in the way everybody knows what a shipwreck is without ever having seen the sea. There was a word and the word was *Welling*, and it meant a place where the Deep came up through the Sill, and it meant Wildborn, and it meant a licence and a fence and a man with a book, and it meant — if it was left alone long enough — a surge, which is the word for what happens to a village that had one and did not know.

There was a Welling under Hollow Alder. Four hundred yards from the last house. Under the trees the village was named for. On ground that ran at three knots for forty acres in every direction and had never in eight hundred years grown anything worse than a badger.

He was fourteen years old and he had found it with a candle and a piece of twine because he had wanted to cheat at his own kindling.

The correct thing to do was to walk to Marrowfield, which was three days, and go into the Ledger office and say what he had found. They would send someone. They would fence it and licence it and Dorn Vance would come out with the grey eyes and stand in the alders and take charge of it, and it would be handled properly by people who knew what they were doing, and nobody in Hollow Alder would be eaten.

That was not a hard question. It was not even a question. Wick did not spend one second of that hour pretending it was a question.

What he spent the hour on was the other thing, which was that he was going to go down there first.

Not instead. *First.*

He worked at it for a long time and he could not make it come out clean. He tried the version where he told himself the village was safe for a fortnight. That one held up, actually, and he distrusted it for exactly that reason. He tried the version where he told himself Vance would seal it and there would be nothing left, and he was fairly sure that was true and he noticed how much he wanted it to be.

In the end he stopped trying to make it clean, because it was not going to be, and he sat in the tree with the cold coming up past his knees and said the thing out loud, which was the only decent part of the whole business.

"I'm going down there because I want to," he said. "Not because it's right."

The alder did not have an opinion.

"And I'll go to Marrowfield after," he said. "Before Gather. That's a promise, and it's a real one, and if I break it I'll know I broke it."

He climbed down out of the tree. His hands were shaking, from the cold, mostly.

Then he went home and had his dinner and said nothing at all, which was, he discovered, remarkably easy, and which frightened him more than the hole had.


---

# Chapter 7 — A Seam

He went down on the twenty-sixth of Green, in the afternoon, because he had worked out that if something went wrong at night nobody would come looking until morning and if something went wrong in the afternoon nobody would come looking until morning either, but at least he would be able to see.

He tied the rope to the second alder, which was solid, and not the third, which was not. He put the tinder-box in his shirt and a stub of candle in each pocket and a third in his boot. He took the spear, which was seven feet long and immediately turned out to be the stupidest object anybody had ever tried to take down a hole, and left it wedged in the split of the tree where he could reach it coming up.

Then he went in feet first with the rope in both hands and his elbows against the chalk and slid.

---

The first thing was that the cold stopped.

It stopped about fifteen feet down, all at once, the way stepping out of a draught stops. Above that line the air came up cold and washed and smelling of nothing. Below it the air was warm and wet and smelled overwhelmingly of *something*, and the something was mushrooms and old fat and the inside of a mouth.

Wick hung on the rope with his boots braced and breathed it and did not like it at all.

Then he looked down past his own feet, and the shaft opened out, and he forgot about the smell.

---

Being an Ember meant you could see the Wild.

That was the whole of it, at Ember. You did not get a working and you did not get a strong back; you got a fire in a small room and, from the moment you woke up, you could see. It had taken Wick about a week to stop noticing it and then a week more to understand what he had stopped noticing. The Wild lay over Hollow Alder like a faint grain in the air, a very thin haze that moved when the wind moved and pooled in the cellar and thinned on the ridge, and after fourteen days it was simply what the world looked like, and he could no more have described it than he could have described the colour green.

He had been looking at three knots his entire life.

The shaft opened out into a chamber the size of the Hollow's taproom, and the Wild in it was so thick that he could not see the far wall through it.

It was not haze. It was *water*. It hung and turned in slow ropes and sheets, pale as skimmed milk, and it moved on its own with nothing moving it, and where it lay along the floor it was two feet deep and it ran, sluggishly, downhill, into a passage at the far end and away.

Wick came off the rope onto a floor of soft chalk and stood in it up to his shins and could feel it. Not with his hands. With the front of his chest, behind the ribs, where the salt was, a slow pressure like standing in a river.

He got the candle lit on the fourth try because his hands were unhelpful.

The flame came up round.

Not a teardrop. A ball, the size of a marble, sitting on the wick, perfectly still and perfectly spherical, and it gave off about four times the light a candle had any business giving off, and it burned steadily and did not consume any wax at all in the entire time he stood there staring at it.

"Right," Wick said, and his voice went out flat and dead into the chamber and came back off nothing.

---

The chalk had been eaten.

That was the only way to describe what had happened to the walls. Chalk was chalk; you got it in the Loam under a spade's depth everywhere, white and dry and crumbly and dull. This chalk had gone soft and slightly clear at the edges, like tallow, like the fat off the top of a stockpot when it sets, and it had run. There were shapes in the walls that had been made by something running very slowly for a very long time: ledges and lips and long hanging drips, all of it faintly translucent, all of it giving back the candle in a way that made the room look lit from inside.

There was water coming through from above. He found where: a seam in the roof that was letting the Wickwater in a drop at a time. That answered the third alder holding low. The river was leaking into this.

And there were things growing.

That was the part he had not been prepared for, and it was the part he would still be thinking about when he was three hundred years old.

There was a patch of grass on the floor of the chamber, about nine feet across.

It was white, because it had never seen the sun, and it was ankle-high and perfectly even, and it grew out of soft chalk in a place with no light. Wick walked over to it with his round candle held out and crouched down and touched it and it was grass. Cold, springy, ordinary grass. He pulled a blade and it broke the way grass breaks.

Nine feet across. He paced it, and then he paced it again, because a guess and a number are different things.

Nine feet by nine feet, near enough, and the Wild lying over that nine feet was *not moving* while the rest of the room's turned like smoke.

There was a room in the floor. Not a Hold. Nobody owned it. It was a room-sized piece of somewhere-else, sitting in the chalk, growing grass in the dark, and it had got there without anyone kindling it or planting it or wanting it.

Wick sat down on his heels in the middle of it with his arms round his knees.

Everything he knew about the practice, everything the whole village knew, came down to a person having a place inside them. That was the arrangement. The world was out here and the terrain was in there, and Seeds were how you moved a bit of one into the other, and that was the deal and it was the same deal for everybody.

This was terrain lying loose on the ground.

"You just *grow*," he said out loud, to the grass. "Nobody's got you at all."

He had, at that moment, no way of knowing that he was sitting in the middle of the single most important fact of his life, or that he would spend sixty years working out what it meant. What he had was the strong, unhelpful, entirely accurate sense that the arrangement he had been told about was not the arrangement, and that everybody in Hollow Alder was doing arithmetic with the wrong numbers.

Then something moved in the passage.

---

He got behind a chalk lip and put the candle out with his fingers, which hurt, and did not make a sound.

It came along the floor through the Wild, and the Wild went round it the way water goes round a stone.

It was about the length of his forearm and it had no bones in it. It moved by putting a fold of itself forward and pouring the rest along after, and it was pale and wet and it left a track behind it, and every few feet it stopped and lifted its front end and turned it slowly through half a circle, and there was nothing on the front end. No eyes. Nothing. It was smooth.

Wick had heard the words *tallow-crawler* his whole life the way he had heard *shipwreck*. Four copper for the Seed and threepence for the head. A stomach and no sense of smell. Hob had made it a joke and Wick had done arithmetic about thirty-eight of them without once picturing one.

It came up onto the grass and stopped.

Then it went round the grass.

It did not cross the patch. It came to the white grass and stopped dead and put its front up and turned it through half a circle and then it went round the whole nine feet, close to the edge, all the way, and got back on its line and carried on toward the shaft.

Toward the rope.

Wick did not breathe for a while.

It went past the bottom of the rope without any interest in it and put its front end against the wall and started up. Not fast. It went up the soft chalk about twelve feet, and stopped, and hung there, and did nothing at all.

He counted to two hundred. It did nothing.

He understood, slowly and with real unhappiness, that it was in the shaft, and that it was between him and the outside, and that it was not doing anything because it did not need to do anything, and that he had come down a hole with three candles and no working and had left the spear at the top like an idiot.

The Wild ran past his shins, sluggish, warm, ten feet deep in the corners, more Wild in this one room than in the entire village he had grown up in, and he could not use a single drop of it, because he had no fire and forty-one acres of salt and there was nothing whatsoever inside him to do it with.

He sat down behind the chalk lip in the dark and made himself think.

---

He got out an hour and a half later, and the way he got out was this.

He took the candle stub out of his boot and lit it, low down behind the lip, and rolled it across the floor to the far side of the chamber, where it went on burning with its little round unnatural flame and lit up eleven feet of wall.

Nothing happened for a long time.

Then the thing on the shaft wall came down and went across the floor toward the light, and while it was going across the floor Wick went up the rope hand over hand with his boots skidding on the chalk and his heart going like a bird in a box, and he came out through the base of the third hollow alder into the ordinary cold afternoon and lay on the gravel bar in the daylight for a good ten minutes.

He was shaking. He noted that with some interest.

He was not, he found, frightened. Or he was, and it was underneath something else, and the something else was so large that he had to sit up and put his hands on his knees and look at the river for a while before he could name it.

There was a piece of the world down there that nobody owned.

There was a nine-foot room lying on the floor of it growing grass in the dark, and a thing with no eyes that had gone round it rather than over it, and enough Wild in one chamber to drown the whole of Hollow Alder, and it was four hundred yards from his gran's kitchen and it had been there, probably, for longer than the village.

And he could still, right now, this afternoon, walk up the road and get on Hob Tunny's cart on Tuesday and be in Marrowfield by Friday and go into the Ledger office and say: *there's a Welling under the ford*.

That was still available. He turned it over honestly and it was completely available. He would be the boy who found it. Somebody would probably give him a shilling. Vance would come out with the grey eyes and take charge and fence it and it would be handled properly by people who knew what they were doing, and that would be that, and it would be *right*, and Wick would go back to the barley.

He sat on the gravel bar until the light started to go.

Then he got up and untied the rope from the second alder and coiled it, and got the spear down out of the split of the tree, and stood for a moment looking at seven feet of ash with a boar head on it, thinking about a passage four feet wide and a chalk lip and a thing the length of his forearm that had no eyes on the front of it.

"Too long," he said.

And that was the moment, although he did not notice it going past. Not the promise in the tree. Not the decision. It was standing on a gravel bar at dusk with a coil of rope over his shoulder, holding a spear, thinking about *length*.

He walked home and put the spear back on its pegs and ate his dinner and answered his father's questions about the ford, which were three, and which he answered truthfully by saying that the water was very cold and that he had been looking at the alders.

Then he went out to the byre and found the hand-axe and a whetstone and sat down on an upturned bucket, and began, without any real idea of what he was doing, to think about how a person might make something four feet long out of something seven feet long.


---

# Chapter 8 — Four Feet of Ash

The spear had been his grandfather's and it had killed, by family reckoning, nine boar, and Wick cut two and a half feet off it in the byre on a Tuesday evening with a hand-axe and a rasp and no permission whatsoever.

He was sick about it for a full minute afterward, sitting on the bucket with the offcut in his hands. Then he got on with it, because being sick about a thing you have already done is a waste of an evening.

He rounded the new butt and burned it hard in the fire so it would not split, and he sat with the whetstone until the head would take the hair off his arm, and he wrapped the grip in twine because his hands had sweated on it in the chalk and he had not liked that. It came out at four feet and a bit, and it was heavy in the wrong place now, and it was still the best thing he owned.

Gran watched him do the last of it from the doorway with a lamp.

"Your grandad's," she said.

"Yes."

"Mm," said Gran Alder, and went to bed.

---

He went down on the second of Long with the short spear, a lantern instead of candles, and a plan he had spent four nights building and was rather proud of.

The plan was this: tallow-crawlers went toward light. He had seen one do it. So he would put the lantern in the middle of the floor, get behind the chalk lip with the spear, and when one came to the lantern he would put four feet of ash through it from above and behind while it was busy.

It was a good plan. Every part of it was correct. He had observed the behaviour, formed the theory, arranged the ground, and chosen the moment, and if you had put that plan in front of any hunter in the Loam they would have said *well, yes, that will do,* and Wick spent a long time afterward being grateful that he had got it right, because it meant the lesson he learned was a real one and not just the ordinary lesson about being fourteen.

He set the lantern down. He got behind the lip. He waited an hour and forty minutes with his knees going to stone, watching pale rope of Wild turn slowly through the room and pour away down the passage.

Then one came, and went to the lantern, and stopped, and lifted its front end and turned it through half a circle, and Wick came up out of the dark and put the spear through the middle of it with all the weight he had.

The head went in. It went in easily, the whole width of the thing, and out the other side, and struck the chalk floor beneath, and stuck.

And nothing happened.

---

Nothing happened for perhaps a second and a half, which was long enough for Wick to understand, with a horrible clarity, every part of what he had got wrong.

He had put a spear through a boar. That was a thing he had done, with his father, twice. You put a spear through a boar and it *stops*, because a boar has a spine and lungs and a heart, and a spear finds one of them or it opens the boar wide enough that the boar stops caring about you.

A tallow-crawler had none of those things. He had known that. Hob had said *boneless* and Wick had heard the word and had filed it and had not, at any point in four nights of planning, actually thought about what it meant.

What it meant was that he had made a hole in it.

The thing did not scream, because it had nothing to scream with. It did not even convulse. It simply flowed — the whole pale length of it moved at once, sideways, off the spear, the way a spilled thing moves off a slope, and it came off the head and went round the shaft in a fold and it was on the haft, on the wood, coming up.

Wick let go of the spear.

That was the right decision and he made it in about a quarter of a second and it did not help, because it had already crossed onto his hands.

There was no weight to it. That was the thing his body could not get past. He had braced for something like a dog and there was nothing there, no mass, no push, just a cold wet sleeve arriving up his forearm faster than he could pull back, and then a sensation in the meat below his elbow like being pressed very hard with a thumb.

He hit it with the lantern.

He did not decide to. His left hand had the lantern in it and his left hand swung, and the lantern glass went and the oil went and the flame went with it, and there was a smell like a rendering shed, and the thing came off his arm all at once and poured onto the floor and away, burning in two places, into the passage.

The lantern went out.

---

It is about twenty feet from the floor of that chamber to the base of the third hollow alder and Wick went up it in the dark on a rope with one arm that worked properly, and he did not remember any of it afterward at all.

He came out through the rot at the bottom of the tree and got maybe six feet along the gravel bar before he stopped and was extremely sick into the Wickwater.

Then he sat with his back against the second alder and held his right arm up to the moon and looked at it.

There were two marks below the elbow. They were not much. They were the size and shape of a thumbprint each, side by side, and they were not bleeding, and the skin over them was not even broken in any way that showed. They were faintly grey at the edges.

He pressed one. It did not hurt.

That was, he found out over the following four days, considerably worse than if it had.

---

He got home at midnight and washed his arm at the trough for a long time, and went to bed, and by morning the two marks had gone from grey at the edges to grey in the middle and the arm ached from wrist to shoulder in a deep unlocatable way, like a tooth.

By the second morning he could not close his hand properly and there were red lines going up the inside of his forearm, thin and neat, following the veins.

He did the water. He did the ewes. He wore his sleeves down in the second week of Long, in warm weather, in a village where every man in the fields had his sleeves rolled past the elbow, and he told himself that nobody had noticed, and Gran had noticed on the first evening.

She let it run four days.

Wick thought about that a great deal in later years and never entirely got to the bottom of it. It was not that she had not known what to do. Gran Alder had been putting things on wounds since before his father was born. It was, he eventually concluded, that she had wanted him to come and say it, and that she had watched him not say it for four days, and had made a judgement about what kind of person he was going to be, and had then gone and got the vinegar anyway.

On the fourth evening she was sitting on the bench with the bowl and the cloth and the vinegar already out when he came in from the field.

"Sit down," she said.

"Gran —"

"Sit *down*, Wick."

He sat down. She took his wrist and pushed the sleeve up to the elbow and looked at what was there, and her face did not change at all, and she was quiet for a good while.

"Well," she said. "It's not a dog."

"No."

"It's not a rat, and it's not a nail, and it's not a horse, and I've seen all three." She turned the arm over. "Two marks, side by side, no puncture, grey in the middle, and the red gone up the veins in four days and not two, which means whatever it was, it isn't in a hurry." She looked up. "That's a bite."

"Yes."

"From what?"

And Wick, who had lied by omission at his own dinner table twice a day for three weeks and had found it disturbingly easy, found that he could not do it once when she asked him a straight question with his arm in her hands.

"There's a Welling under the ford," he said.

---

She did not shout. He had expected shouting and he had rehearsed for it and he had nothing at all prepared for what actually happened, which was that his grandmother put his arm down carefully on her knee, and sat back, and said, "How long?"

"I found it on the twenty-fourth of Green."

"Nineteen days."

"Yes."

"And you've been down it."

"Twice."

"Mm."

She got up and went inside. Wick sat on the bench with his arm aching and the light going and had time to run through every possible thing she could be fetching, and it was none of them: she came back out with the vinegar bowl and a candle and a small iron thing like a pair of blunt tongs, and she sat back down and started work on his arm without another word, and it hurt so much that he had to hold on to the bench with his other hand.

"Now," said Gran Alder, working. "I'm going to say a number of things, and you'll not interrupt, because this is going to take a while and I want it all said before I lose my temper, which will be in about ten minutes."

"Yes, Gran."

"First. You'll go to Marrowfield and you'll report it."

"I know. I said I would. I said it out loud, to myself, in the tree, and I meant it, and I'm going before Gather."

"Before *Gather*." She pressed the cloth on and he made a noise. "Gather's five weeks."

"Yes."

"There are three hundred people up that road, and a hole with things in it four hundred yards down it, and you've made a private arrangement with a tree." Her voice had not risen at all, which was much worse. "That's the first thing and I've said it. Second."

She stopped working and looked at him.

"You'll not go down there again with that arm."

"Gran —"

"That's not me forbidding you. I gave up forbidding you the day you were nine." She went back to the cloth. "That's me telling you a fact about your arm, which is that it's got something in it that isn't blood, and it's four days in and it's gone up past the elbow, and if you go down a hole with it and you sweat, you'll be lying at the bottom of that hole by morning with your gran not knowing where to look. That's not a rule. That's just what'll happen."

Wick looked at the two grey marks.

"How long?" he said.

"Vinegar and a poultice and rest, and it'll be out of you in ten days."

"Ten."

"Ten. And you'll drink what I give you and you'll not like it." She tied the cloth off. "Third thing."

She sat back and folded her hands.

"You cut your grandad's spear down to four foot," she said, "and I've known for a fortnight, and I've said nothing, and do you know why?"

"No."

"Because a boy who's given up doesn't measure anything, and he doesn't cut down a spear, and he doesn't sit in a byre for four hours getting the balance wrong and doing it again." She looked out at the yard. "I've watched people give up in this village all my life. It doesn't look like crying. It looks like sitting still and being pleasant."

She got up and picked up the bowl.

"So I'm not going to tell you to stop," said Gran Alder. "I'm going to tell you that you're going about it like a boy who thinks he's the only one who's ever been frightened, and that you're going to get killed at it, and that when you go back down there — and you will, and we both know it, and I'll not insult either of us by pretending otherwise — you'll go down knowing what you're doing, and not on a plan you made up in a byre out of one thing you saw once."

"I don't know how to do that."

"No," Gran agreed. "You don't. That's the whole of your trouble, and it's not a small one." She went to the door and stopped there. "So go and find out. There's a man comes through here every week with a cart and a mouth on him, and he's been in and out of Marrowfield for twenty years, and you've asked him about money twice and about killing nothing."

The door shut.

Wick sat on the bench in the dark with his arm strapped and throbbing and the vinegar smell coming off it, and thought about a spear going through something and coming out the other side and making a hole, and how a hole in a boar is a wound and a hole in a spilled thing is nothing at all.

*You have to take it apart,* he thought. *Or you have to change what it's made of.*

He had ten days, and he could not use his right arm, and he had, at that exact moment, no idea what he was going to do.

He got a piece of the feed ledger out anyway, and a stub of charcoal, and sat there in the dark writing down, left-handed, in a hand that was even worse than his usual, a list of every single thing he had seen a tallow-crawler do.

There were eleven items. He had not known there were eleven.


---

# Chapter 9 — Eleven Things

By the third day of the poultice the list had thirty-one items on it, because once you start writing down what you actually saw rather than what you remember seeing, it turns out you saw a great deal.

Wick kept it on the back of the feed ledger with everything else, in charcoal, left-handed, and he read it over so many times that he could recite it for the rest of his life.

> 1. Moves by folding a bit of the front forward and pouring the rest after.
> 2. No eyes. No face. Front end turns through half a circle and stops.
> 3. Goes toward a lantern. Went toward a candle too. Went slowly both times.
> 4. **Went round the white grass. Did not go over it.** Went all the way round, close in, and got back on its line.
> 5. Leaves a wet track that stays wet.
> 6. A hole in it does nothing at all.
> 7. Came off the spear sideways, the way spilled fat comes off a slope.
> 8. Got onto my arm faster than I could pull back and weighed nothing.
> 9. Bite: two marks like thumbprints, no cut, grey in the middle, four days to the elbow.
> 10. Burning oil took it off me at once. It went away burning in two places and it kept going.
> 11. Cold and wet, in a room that is warm and wet.

Items twelve through thirty-one were smaller and duller and some of them were probably wrong, and item four had a box drawn round it because it was the one he could not explain and could not stop looking at.

He had been very sure, on the first day, that fire was the answer. Item ten said so. Fire had come off well.

By the fourth day he had gone off it. Fire had made the thing let go of his arm, which was not the same as killing it, and it had gone away *still moving* with two parts of itself alight, and he had a strong suspicion that a creature which flows would simply put itself out by flowing, and in any case the only fire he had was a lantern, and the lantern was broken, and it had cost eleven pence and he did not have eleven pence.

On the sixth day, still one-armed, he walked down to the road and waited two hours for Hob Tunny.

---

"Hob," said Wick, "how do you kill a tallow-crawler?"

Hob Tunny stopped with the nosebag half on.

"Do what?"

"How do you kill one? Properly. Not with a spear."

"Why in God's name would you want to know that?"

Wick had prepared for this and his preparation was, he was slightly ashamed to notice, extremely good.

"Because I'm going to be told I'm Failed of Kindling in Rust," he said, "and everyone in this village has been kind to me for six weeks, and I've decided I'd rather know things than be kind to." He shrugged. "And you're the only person I know who's ever been near a Hall."

Hob looked at him for a moment. Then he took the nosebag right off, which meant Wick had an hour.

"Right," said Hob Tunny. "Well. First off, nobody kills a crawler with a spear, and anybody who tells you they have is telling you about a different animal. You can't stab a thing that's got no inside." He held up a finger. "Second, you can't chase one. You'd think you could. Great slow horrible thing, you'd think a man could just walk after it. You can't, because it goes down a crack, and it can go down a crack the width of your thumb, and it'll come out somewhere else in an hour with you still standing there."

"So what do they do?"

"Salt," said Hob.

Wick did not say anything. He had, in fact, stopped being able to for a second.

"Crawler-salt," Hob went on, entirely unaware. "You can buy it in any Hall in the Loam, penny the pound, and it's not crawler-salt at all, it's *salt*, and the Hall knows it and you know it and they charge you a penny anyway because they've got you. Coarse ground. You lay a ring of it about so wide" — he made a circle with his arms, four feet across — "on good dry ground, and you put something in the middle of it they want, and then you go and sit down."

"And?"

"And nothing. That's the whole of it. It comes for the bait and it gets to the salt and it stops, because it won't cross salt, not for anything. So it goes round. And round. And there's no round to go, because it's a ring." Hob spread his hands. "And after a while it tries to get across anyway, because they're not clever, and then it's finished, because salt takes the wet out of it and the wet is all it's got. Takes about the time to smoke a pipe."

"It won't cross salt."

"Won't cross salt, won't cross a drawn line of it, won't cross a scattering." Hob shrugged. "That's not clever hunting, mind. Any fool can lay a ring. That's why crawlers are four copper. Bottom of the board, that is. The men who take crawler writs are the ones who've got a bad leg or a bad reputation."

Wick was doing arithmetic and it was not going well.

"How much salt for a ring?"

"Ten pound. Twelve if the ground's rough."

"That's a shilling."

"That's a shilling," Hob agreed, "and you get four copper for the Seed, so you'd want to be picking your salt up after and using it twice, and you would, and everybody does, and that's why the Halls sell it in a sack with a drawstring." He grinned. "Nobody's getting rich off crawlers, boy."

"And the Seed?"

"Middle of it. Bout the size of a pin-head, grey, and greasy, and you want a knife and no imagination." He put the nosebag back on. "And you want to be quick, because they go off. Half a day and the Seed's spoiled and you've got a shilling of salt spent on a bad smell."

---

He had, in the whole world, four pence.

He sat in the yard that evening and did the sums and did not like any of them. A shilling was twelve pence. Twelve pence was three times everything he owned. He could ask his father, and his father would give it to him, and his father would then be short twelve pence at Gather when they were already short nine hundred and twelve.

Then he stopped, and put the charcoal down, and looked across the yard at the byre, where there was a lump of rock salt in a wooden trough the size of a loaf of bread, because every farm in the Loam that had ever kept sheep had a lump of rock salt in a trough, because sheep need salt and it costs a shilling a stone and lasts two years.

Wick sat and looked at it for a while.

Then he laughed out loud, once, in the empty yard.

---

It took him four days to break it up, because it was rock salt and because he had one arm.

He did it in the byre with the back of the hand-axe and a flat stone, an hour at a time, and it was the most tedious work he had ever done, and the salt got in the two grey marks below his elbow and stung so badly the first time that he sat down.

By the end of it he had a flour sack about a third full, coarse-ground, grey-white, and gritty, and it weighed, on his father's cheese scale, a little over six pounds.

Six.

He needed ten.

He sat in the byre doorway with the sack between his feet and turned it over for a long time. He could take more from the trough. He could take the whole trough, and the ewes would go without salt for a season and would be ill, and his father would find out and would not shout and would look at him in the way he had looked at him about the spear, which was worse.

He could go and get more chalk-salt off the road, except there was no such thing.

He could use less.

Wick got the feed ledger out and read item four for what was probably the eight hundredth time.

> 4. **Went round the white grass. Did not go over it.** Went all the way round, close in, and got back on its line.

Nine feet across. Ankle-high, white, growing out of chalk in the dark, and nine feet of Wild lying over it that did not move when the rest of the room's turned like smoke. A room-sized piece of somewhere-else, lying loose on the floor of a Welling, that nobody owned.

And a thing with no eyes had come to the edge of it, and stopped, and gone round.

*It won't cross salt,* Wick thought. *And it won't cross that. And I don't know why it won't cross that, and I don't need to know why yet.*

He got up and paced the byre floor, nine feet, and looked at it.

A ring four feet across needed ten pounds of salt. But he did not need a ring. He needed an *enclosure*, and he had one side of it already, nine feet long, sitting on the floor of that chamber, and it had cost him nothing and it would still be there tomorrow.

If he laid the salt in a horseshoe against the edge of the grass — a curve out from one corner and back to the other — he had a closed shape with two thirds the wall and one third the salt.

He worked it out properly on the ledger, badly, twice, and got six pounds and a bit.

He had six pounds and a bit.

---

He went to bed that night and could not sleep and got up again and went out and sat on the step and drew for three hours, out of pure restlessness, putting cup after cup of nothing into a lake.

And somewhere in the second hour he stopped, halfway through a breath, because something had occurred to him and it would not go away.

He had been thinking about the white grass all week as a wall. It was a wall. It was a very good wall and it was going to save him four pounds of salt and he was pleased with himself about it.

But it was not a wall. It was a piece of terrain, lying on the ground, out in the world, that had *got there*.

And he had forty-one acres with nothing in them.

Wick sat very still on the step in the dark.

He did not have a plan. He did not even have a thought, properly; it was more like the shape of where a thought would go. It sat somewhere behind the arithmetic and it was, roughly: *there is terrain out there, and there is terrain in here, and everybody in the world says the only road between them is a Seed, and I have just spent a week looking at a nine-foot room lying on a floor.*

He did not know what to do with it. He was fourteen, and he could not read properly, and he had six pounds of sheep salt and a cut-down spear and a bad arm.

He wrote it down anyway, on the ledger, under the eleven things, because writing things down was what he had instead of understanding them.

> 32. The grass got there somehow.

---

On the tenth day Gran took the cloth off and looked at his arm and grunted.

The two marks had gone from grey to a dull silvery colour, like old solder, and they were not going to fade; he would have them at eighty. The red lines had gone. He could close his hand.

"Ten days," said Gran Alder.

"You said ten."

"I did." She put the bowl away. "Don't get bit again. It's twice as bad the second time and I don't know why."

He went out to the byre and put the sack of ground rock salt by the door where he had put the coiled rope and the short spear and a candle stub and his father's small knife, which he had cleaned and sharpened and which was going to have to do for a thing the size of a pin-head.

Then he stood in the doorway and looked out at the road, where Ma Dunnock was coming up from the Thatchers' with a face on her.

"Ewe's gone," she said, as she went past. "Off the Rennicks' low field. Gate shut, wall up, and no ewe."

Wick looked at her.

"Foxes," said Ma Dunnock, in the voice of a woman who did not believe it, and went on up the road.

# Chapter 10 — The Warm Damp

It took the time to smoke a pipe, and Wick made himself watch all of it.

---

He went down on the fourteenth of Long with six pounds of sheep salt in a flour sack tied across his back, and it was the third time and it was the first time he was not frightened going in, which he noticed and did not entirely trust.

He laid the horseshoe against the grass. That took him an hour, on his knees in the soft chalk, pouring the salt out of the corner of the sack in a line as thick as his thumb, going out from one corner of the nine-foot patch in a wide curve and back to the other. He had practised it twice in the byre with sand and had learned two things: that his line wandered, and that the difference between a line as thick as a thumb and a line as thick as a finger was two and a half pounds.

When it was done he had a shape on the floor about eleven feet across, with grass on one side of it and salt around the rest, and he had a double handful of salt left in the bottom of the sack, which he tied off and hung on the rope where he could get it.

He put the candle stub in the middle, on a flat of chalk, and lit it.

The flame came up round.

Then he went and got behind the chalk lip and put the short spear across his knees and waited.

---

Wick had done a great deal of waiting in his life. Farm work is mostly waiting arranged around brief periods of panic. He had waited on ewes and on weather and on his father coming back up a field, and he had thought he understood it.

He had not waited in the dark, twenty feet under a river, in a room where the Wild lay two feet deep on the floor and turned in slow ropes and poured away down a passage, for a thing that had put its mouth on his arm eleven days ago.

Two hours in, he began to be able to hear his own pulse, and it took him another half hour to work out that it was not his pulse, it was the drip through the seam in the roof, and that it was exactly the speed of a slow heartbeat and had been the whole time.

Two hours and forty minutes in, one came.

---

It came out of the passage on the far side and it took a while to arrive, the way they did: fold and pour, fold and pour, stopping every few feet to lift the front end and turn it through half a circle.

Wick watched it come and had time to notice a thirty-third thing, which was that when it stopped and turned, the Wild lying on the floor moved. Not much. It went out from the front end in a ring, faintly, like the ring off a dropped stone, and came back.

*It's looking,* he thought. *That's how it looks.*

He filed it. He had no idea what to do with it and he filed it, and it saved his life twice in the next three years.

The thing came on. It reached the near edge of the white grass and stopped, and put its front up, and turned it through half a circle, and the ring went out and came back.

Then it went round.

Wick's hands closed on the spear.

It went round the outside of the grass, close in, exactly as it had done the first time, and it came round the corner where the grass stopped and the salt began, and it went across the corner and into the enclosure, because the salt line started three inches further out than the corner of the grass, and Wick had spent an entire evening in the byre with a piece of chalk working out that if he made the two ends of the horseshoe overlap the grass rather than meet it, there would be a way in and no way out.

The candle sat burning in the middle. It went toward the candle.

Then it stopped, because it had arrived, and there was nothing there to eat.

Wick stayed absolutely still.

The thing lifted its front end and turned it and the ring went out across the floor of the enclosure and hit salt on one side and grass on the other, and it came back, and the tallow-crawler poured itself forward two feet in a new direction and stopped again.

And then it did it again. And again. And it began, slowly and with no sign whatsoever of distress, to go round the inside of the shape, close to the edge, all the way round, and get back to where it had started, and do it again.

It did that eleven times.

---

On the twelfth circuit it tried to cross.

There was no decision in it that Wick could see. It came to the salt at a place it had already come to eleven times and this time it went up onto it, the front fold going out flat over the line, and Wick felt something go out of his chest that he had not known he was holding.

Then it stopped, and it started to come apart.

It was not fast and it was not loud. The front of it, where it had gone over the salt, went dull. That was the first thing: it lost its shine, the way a wet stone loses its shine when the sun gets on it, and then it went from dull to grey to a sort of crumpled dry colour, and it did not pull back.

That was the worst of it. It did not pull back. It could have. It had eleven feet of itself still on clean chalk and it stayed exactly where it was and kept going forward, pouring the wet part of itself into the dry part, and the dry part got bigger.

It took about twenty minutes.

Wick came out from behind the chalk lip after the first three and stood at the edge of the salt line with the spear in his hands and watched the whole rest of it, and there was no reason on earth to do that, and he did it anyway, and he was never able to explain to anybody why except that it had seemed to him, quite strongly, that if he was going to do this then he was going to be the sort of person who looked at it.

At the end there was a thing on the floor about the length of his forearm that was the colour and texture of a wasp's nest, and it did not move, and the room smelled like the inside of a rendering shed.

Wick sat down on the chalk and put his head between his knees for a while.

---

The Seed was where Hob had said it would be, in the middle, and it took him nine minutes to find because the middle of a collapsed tallow-crawler is not an obvious place and he had to open the whole length of it with his father's small knife and go through it with his fingers.

He found it against the last of the wet.

It was the size of a pin-head, exactly as promised. It was grey. It was greasy, and it stayed greasy however much he wiped it, and when he held it up to the round candle-flame it was not quite opaque; there was something in the middle of it that the light went into and did not come out of.

It weighed nothing. He turned it over on his palm and understood, in a distant academic way, that he was holding four copper, and that he was also holding the first thing he had ever owned that was not a farm implement or a piece of string.

He wrapped it in a scrap of the feed ledger and put it in his shirt, against the skin, because he did not trust his pocket.

Then he scraped up every grain of salt he could get, which was about four pounds, because the rest had gone into the floor and into the thing, and he went up the rope one-handed with the sack over his shoulder, and came out through the base of the third hollow alder into a hot bright afternoon in the middle of Long, and sat down on the gravel bar, and found that he was grinning so hard his face hurt.

---

He did not plant it that night. He wanted to and he did not, and holding off was, he thought afterward, the first genuinely disciplined thing he ever did.

He waited two days, and in those two days he asked his father four careful questions and Ma Dunnock two, framed as idle curiosity about how it had gone for Bri, and he learned the following:

You did not need a ceremony. You did not need a registrar. You did not need to be anywhere in particular. You held the Seed in your hand, and you went in, and it came in with you — nobody could tell him how, and nobody had ever thought it strange — and you put it down on the ground, and after that it was not up to you.

"Where'd Bri put hers?" he had asked.

"Corner," his father said. "By the door. That's what they all do. You want it out the way of the fire."

Wick had thought about that for a long time. *That's what they all do.* Nine feet of room and a fire in the middle, and everybody in the world puts their first terrain in the corner by the door, and there is not one person alive who has had to think about where to put a thing in forty-one acres.

---

He went in on the sixteenth of Long, at night, lying on his back in the dark with the Seed in his fist.

The salt was where it always was. The black ceiling of a sky. The silence.

He opened his hand and the Seed was in it, which he had half expected and was still not ready for; a greasy grey pin-head sitting on his palm in a place that had no light in it and yet where he could see perfectly well, which was a thing about his Hold he had stopped questioning around the fourth night.

He stood in the middle and thought about it properly, because he had been thinking about it for two days and he wanted to get it right the first time and there was not going to be a second one.

The middle was the only place he could find.

That was the whole argument and it took him about four seconds to have it. He had forty-one acres and no landmarks and a sky with nothing in it to steer by, and he had exactly one fixed point in the entire country, and it was under his feet, and it was the one place he could walk to from anywhere without knowing where he was.

Everybody else put the first one in the corner by the door, to keep it out of the way of the fire.

He did not have a corner. He did not have a door. He did not have a fire.

Wick Alder knelt down in the middle of forty-one acres of salt and put a pin-head on the ground.

---

Nothing happened for long enough that he began to be sick about it.

Then the salt went dark around his hands.

It went out from the Seed in a ring, slowly, a hand's breadth at a time, and it was not a stain and it was not water; the ground was simply *wet* where it had been dry, and warm where it had been cold, and it kept going, and Wick knelt there with his hands on his knees and watched it go past him on all sides.

It stopped at about two paces across.

Two paces. He stood up and walked round it. It was not even properly round; it had gone further one way than the other for no reason he could see. Two paces of warm wet ground, faintly steaming into cold air, in the middle of forty-one acres.

He had killed a thing and watched it die and cut it open with his father's knife, and had spent four days breaking rock salt one-handed and eleven days with a poisoned arm, and had lied to his family for a month, and what he had to show for it was a damp patch you could cover with a horse blanket.

Wick started laughing and could not stop for a while, and it was not a good laugh at first and then it was.

Because he was standing on it. He was standing on ground that was warm, in a place where he had walked a thousand paces in a straight line and passed nothing, and it had not been there yesterday, and it was going to be there tomorrow.

He got down and put both palms flat on it. It was the temperature of a cow's flank. There was a faint smell, mushrooms and old fat, the inside of a mouth.

Then he sat down cross-legged in the middle of it, on the warm wet ground in the dark under the black ceiling, and breathed out longer than he breathed in, and thought about the bottom of a well.

The thread came in, thin and cool, the way it always did.

It came in through his chest and down through the floor of himself and out onto the ground, and it went — and it did not go far.

It went about a foot and stopped, and sank, and stayed.

Wick opened his eyes.

He put his hand on the ground where it had gone. He could not feel anything. There was nothing to feel; it was warm wet ground and it had been warm wet ground before.

He shut his eyes and did it again, slower.

In through the chest. Down through the floor. Out onto the ground.

And it went about a foot, and sank, and stayed.

He did it eleven more times to be sure, because one was a guess.
