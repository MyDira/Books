# Chapter 1 — Four Copper

The going rate for a tallow-crawler had not moved in eleven years and Wick Alder had killed two hundred and six of them since the Slack.

He did the two hundred and seventh at about four in the afternoon on the ninth of Seed, forty feet down a chalk seam under a hill that had a licence nailed to a post at the top of it, and it took him nine seconds.

The method was this. He put his left hand flat on the wall of the seam and pushed a film of warm wet ground out through the palm, and the chalk went dark in a patch the size of a dinner plate, and the crawler came to it, because a crawler goes to wet like a moth to a lamp, and there is not one recorded instance of one doing otherwise.

Then he pulled the water back.

The patch of chalk went from soaked to dry to a hard pale glaze in about a second and a half, with the front third of a tallow-crawler set into it like a fly in tallow, and the rest of the animal went on pouring itself forward into the dry because that is what they do, and after nine seconds there was a crumpled thing on the floor the colour of a wasp's nest and Wick was already looking at the next bit of wall.

"Two hundred and seven," said Nettle, from behind him, not looking up from the ledger.

"Seven."

"That's four copper."

"That's four copper."

"You are the best in this parish at a thing worth four copper," said Nettle, "and I have decided to find that funny, because the alternative is available and I have looked at it."

---

The writ was *TALLOW, SEAM 4, INFESTATION — Ember grade — 4d the Seed, party rates on application*, and it had been on the board at the Marrowfield Hall since before he could read it.

He had stood in front of that board in Dust with his mouth moving, unlicensed, unranked, barred from touching any of it. Nettle had told him there was a rule about standing there and there had not been.

Now he could take it, and it was the only kind he could take, and that was the whole of the arrangement.

The tally said it in eleven words on the back, stamped, where the clerk had put it in a hand so neat it looked printed:

> *Hearth. Held on the boundary. Weight 0.6. See appended note.*

A Hearth might take a Hearth writ. A Hearth held on the boundary took Ember writs at Ember pay, and there was no argument about it and no appeal and no hardship provision, and the reason there was none was that in four hundred years the Ledger had used the status about twice in a generation and had never once had cause to think it might be unfair.

Chalk-hound: two shillings and a three-shilling bounty. He could not take one.
Tallow-crawler: four copper and threepence for the head, and a man with a bad leg or a bad reputation.

Two hundred and six of them, so far.

---

They worked seam four until the light went out of the shaft, which at that depth was a formality, and came up at six with forty-one Seeds in a drawstring bag and the specific smell that gets into wool and stays.

Hall clerk weighed the bag, counted the heads, and wrote it in the book.

"Forty-one at fourpence. Thirteen and eight."

"Thank you."

"Party rates?"

"No party," said Nettle. "One licensed member, one on the boundary, no beaters."

The clerk's pen paused about a quarter of an inch above the page, and Wick watched him decide not to say the thing he had been going to say, and be pleased with himself about it, and write the figure down.

Thirteen shillings and eightpence, for two days at the bottom of a chalk seam.

Dorn Vance, who held the licence for the whole circuit and who was the reason Wick had the paper at all, paid a Hall man **nine shillings a day** and expenses.

---

"Say it," said Nettle, outside, in the yard behind the Hall, where they had had a great many of their conversations because it had a water butt and nobody in it.

"Say what?"

"You've been doing arithmetic since the counter. You go quiet in a particular way." She put her back against the wall. "So say it, and then we can go and eat something, because if you carry it about all evening you'll be unbearable at me instead."

Wick sat down on the butt.

"Thirteen and eight for two days," he said. "That's about six and ten a day for the pair of us, which is three and five each, which is a bit over a third of what the Hall pays a man to do the same job an hour up that hill."

"And?"

"And it's not the money."

"No," Nettle agreed.

"It's that there isn't a way up." He turned his hands over and looked at them; the knuckles were cracked across where the crust set and broke, and had been since Turn, and were going to be. "I can't take a Hearth writ till I'm Hearth in full. I can't be Hearth in full till my weight's one and a half. Weight comes from drawing, and drawing goes into settled ground, and I've got nought point zero zero one four of an acre settled out of forty-three point nine."

"You've got fifty-two breaths a night."

"I've got sixty-one now. It went up when the rim went up." He said it without any pleasure. "And at sixty-one breaths a night I get to one and a half in about — " he did it, and did not enjoy doing it — "nine years."

Nettle was quiet.

"Nine years of four copper," said Wick.

---

Here is what Marrowfield was like that winter, and it is worth setting down, because he was there for the whole of it and because a great deal of what came later was decided by a boy of fifteen being mildly and continuously humiliated in a town of four thousand people.

Nobody was cruel to him. That was the thing he could never explain to Gran in a letter.

The Hall clerks were polite. The bath-house let him use the good end. The bonesetter did his cracked knuckles for nothing twice and then charged him, which was correct. Men he did not know bought him a drink on the strength of a story about a village and a bag of salt, and then asked him what his weight was, and he told them, and they said *ah* and were kind about it.

*Ah.* He heard that syllable eleven times a week for four months.

There was a boy called Marrin who worked the barrows with a Hall party and who was seventeen and Hearth in full at two and one, and who could not read a Wild-density profile to save his life, and who was perfectly friendly, and who earned nine shillings a day.

Marrin once said to him, meaning it well, "You want to get your weight up," and Wick said "Yes," and they talked about something else.

He wrote none of this to his grandmother. What he wrote was that the work was steady and the town was loud and Nettle had found a room over a chandler's with a stove in it, and all of that was true, and Gran wrote back a single line at the bottom of Tam's letter that said *You'll not tell me the rest, so I'll ask when you're home*, and he read it four times and put it in the box with the other one.

---

He went in that night, in a rented room over a chandler's on Cross Street, with the noise of a town going on underneath the window, and did the two things he did every night without exception.

**First**, he walked the rim. Twenty-eight paces round the warm damp, entering from a bearing he had not used the night before, tallied on the ledger so that he could not repeat himself. Ninety-one circuits that night; he had got faster.

The rim was two hands high now and hard as a flowerpot and it had a bow in it on the north side that was never coming out.

**Second**, he paced the seam.

He did it the same way every night, out from the middle on a bearing, counting, and it was there where it always was, a hard line about a hand across running away in both directions further than he could see, and where it ran, the salt was not salt any more and nothing would grow in it and nothing ever would.

Before that, though, he did the thing he had done on the first of every month since Long, which was to pace the warm damp itself.

Nine paces across. It had been nine paces across since the second of Gather, when he had put a load on his own wall and it had failed at the one place he had been careless, and the water had gone out across the salt and taken two paces of ground with it.

He paced it.

**Nine and a half.**

Wick stood in the middle of it with his hands at his sides.

Then he paced it again, from a different bearing, because one measurement is a guess.

Nine and a half.

And again, and again, from four corners, and it came out nine and a half, nine and a half, a shade over nine and a half, and nine and a half.

He came out and sat on the edge of the bed and thought about it for the length of time it takes to be sensible.

He had not planted anything since Gather. He had forty-one crawler Seeds in a drawstring bag in the Hall's strongbox and he had not put one of them in the ground, because he had made a rule in Dark that he would not plant a single thing until he understood why the last one had cost him two paces, and he had kept it, and it had been the most annoying four months of his life.

So it had not grown. Nothing had been added to it. It could not have grown.

*You have miscounted,* he thought, *because you are tired, and because it is easier to have miscounted than to have a country that does things when you are not looking.*

He wrote **9½?** on the ledger with a question mark, and underneath it, *check on the first of Green*, and then he went back to the thing that actually frightened him.

**Nine paces and a half from the middle.**

He came out and lit the candle and got the ledger and wrote it in the column under the date, and then sat looking at the column, which had run down four pages since Gather and which he could now recite.

Fifteen at the start. Eleven and a quarter at the assessment. Ten and a half at the Slack. Nine and a half tonight.

Six months. Five and a half paces.

It was slowing. It went on slowing. He had plotted it out on the back of a Hall notice in Dark, dot by dot, and the shape of it was the shape of everything else in that country. It went and it went and it slowed while it went, exactly as the survey line had, exactly as the pegs had: nine and seven and five and three and two and one and a half.

He had done the arithmetic on that curve in Dark and had got an answer he liked, and had checked it twice, and had written it down.

> *At this rate it does not reach the rim. It gets close and it keeps getting closer and it never gets there. Like the pegs. It is asymptotic. WORK OUT WHETHER YOU BELIEVE THIS OR WHETHER YOU WANT IT.*

He had underlined the last sentence twice.

Then, because he had promised himself in Rust that he would stop pretending a thing was measured when it was guessed, he had written underneath:

> *You have got four points and a nice shape. Four is a number. Four is not a LAW.*

---

The other thing he did that night was the thing he did not write down.

He got the letters out of the box. There were two. The first was the last one Bri had ever sent, from Y-810, two years and four months ago, and it was about a girl called Sen who worked at the same place as her and who had a laugh like a gate, and it was four lines long and it did not say what the place was.

The second was not a letter. It was the brown paper off the third parcel, the one with the eight shillings in it, folded flat, with *Alder, Whitts, Hollow Alder* on the front in a good clear hand.

Four months in Marrowfield since the Slack, and he had asked in nine places.

At the Ledger office a clerk had told him, correctly and not unkindly, that the Ledger does not tell you where people are, and that if it did nobody would ever file anything true again.

Three chandlers, two carters, the Hall, and the Concord front office on Fell Street, where a woman had taken Bri's name and gone away and come back and said that there was no journeyman of that name on the register, which was true and was also not the question he had asked.

He put the brown paper back in the box.

Four months. Nine places. A town of four thousand, and a girl who had got thirteen feet of brick at fourteen and had walked out of Hollow Alder with a bag and a licence-application, and who had sent money three times in two years and no word at all.

She was here. Hob had carried the parcels. They had gone on the Marrowfield mail.

"Right," said Wick Alder, to a rented room over a chandler's.

Then he blew the candle out, and lay listening to four thousand people being loud in the dark, and had absolutely no idea that in nine days a man would walk up to him in the yard behind the Hall of Hunt and offer to sell him his sister's address for two shillings.

# Chapter 2 — The Address

The man who sold him his sister came up to him in the yard behind the Hall on the eighteenth of Seed and opened with, "You're the salt boy."

"I'm Wick Alder."

"That's what I said." He was about thirty and had a Hall runner's satchel and the specific cheerfulness of a person who is about to ask you for something. "You've been asking after a girl. Alder, out of the Loam, come in about Y-eight-ten."

Wick put down the bag he had been packing.

"I've been asking for four months."

"You've been asking in the wrong nine places," said the man. "Two shilling."

---

His name was Tarrow and he ran parcels and messages between the Hall, the Ledger office, the Concord workshops and about forty private houses, six days a week, and had done for nine years.

He explained his position with great frankness while Wick stood there with two shillings in his hand and had not yet given them to him.

"I'm not selling you a secret. There isn't one." Tarrow leaned on the water butt. "I'm selling you the four months I've already spent. I know where every soul in this town gets their post because I *carry* it, and you've been going round asking clerks, and a clerk will tell you what's on a register and a register only has people on it who are *on* it."

"She's not on the Concord register."

"No."

"I asked at the front office on Fell Street. They said there's no journeyman of that name."

"There isn't," Tarrow agreed. "That's true and they weren't lying to you and you asked the wrong question, and I'd not have thought of the right one either at fifteen." He held his hand out. "Two shilling."

Wick looked at the hand.

Two shillings was a Hall salt sack. It was a third of what he had earned in two days at the bottom of seam four. It was also, and he was aware of this while he stood there, the exact sum Tam Alder had taken out of the crock in Dust and put into his hand at the gate at Whitts, out of Bri's own money, so that he could go to Marrowfield in the first place.

He gave Tarrow the two shillings.

"Sixty-one Fell Street," said Tarrow. "Round the side. There's a green door and a bootscraper shaped like a dog."

"That's the Concord workshops."

"That's the *back* of the Concord workshops." Tarrow put the coins away. "And you'll want to knock at the side and not the front, and you'll want to do it after six, and — " he stopped, and did something with his mouth, and Wick watched a man who made his living being pleasant decide to be honest instead. "Look. I don't know her. I carry a parcel to that door about twice a month and I've had it took off me by a big woman and by a small one, and the small one's got the Loam in her voice and a face a bit like yours."

"That's her."

"That's *probably* her, and I've took your two shilling, and I'd rather you went up that road expecting probably." He shouldered the satchel. "One more thing and it's free."

"Go on."

"She's been there since Y-eight-ten and she's never once been on a register," said Tarrow, "and there's about four reasons for that and only one of them is nice."

---

Fell Street ran up the hill from the market to the barrow road, and the Concord workshops took up most of one side of it: a long frontage of good stone with a brass plate, three storeys, and the particular smell that Wick had learned to identify in his first week and had never got used to, which was cured Seed — sweetish, faintly like a rendering shed, faintly like hot iron.

The front office had a counter and a woman in it who had been perfectly polite to him in Dark.

He did not go to the front office.

Round the side, then: past the coal chute and a stack of empty crates, to a green door with a bootscraper shaped like a dog, and he stood in front of it for an hour and eleven minutes and did not knock.

---

Afterward he worked out that there had been four reasons, and that only one of them was a reason and the other three were furniture.

**The first** was that it was Seed and it was cold and the light had gone, and a man does not knock on a strange door in the dark, and this was nonsense, because it was a quarter past six in the evening in a town with lamps.

**The second** was that Tarrow had said *probably*, and a guess is a guess. This was better nonsense and it lasted him nearly twenty minutes.

**The third** was that he had rehearsed nothing. He had spent four months looking and had not once spent five minutes on what he would actually say, and standing on a bootscraper shaped like a dog he discovered that everything available was either an accusation or a lie.

*Gran wants to know you're all right.* — an accusation.
*I was passing.* — a lie, and a bad one, in a side alley off Fell Street after six.
*You sent eight shillings and no word.* — the truth, and the worst of the three.

**The fourth** was the actual one and it took him until about the fiftieth minute to get to it, and when he got to it he sat down on the crates.

For two years and four months, in a house on thin ground, his family had held a shape in their heads where Bri was, and the shape had been *a girl in a town, doing well enough to send money*. Gran had never once said otherwise. Tam had said *she's all right, then* in a kitchen in Green and had wanted it to be true so much that it had come out as a statement.

And Wick had spent four months telling himself that he was looking for her because Gran had told him to.

He was not. He had worked that out somewhere around the fortieth minute, sitting in an alley with his back against a coal chute, and it was not a nice thing to know about himself.

He was looking for her because he wanted the shape to be *checked*.

He wanted to open a door and find a girl with thirteen feet of brick and a good job and a reason, and take that home to Hollow Alder and hand it over, and be the one who had gone and got it.

And what he was frightened of, standing on a bootscraper, was that he was about to knock on a green door and find something that could not be taken home at all.

At about the sixtieth minute the green door opened.

Wick got off the crates fast and did not manage to look like a person who had a reason to be there, and it did not matter, because the woman who came out did not look at him.

She was enormous. That is the plain fact of it: she was a head taller than any woman in Hollow Alder and built across the shoulders like a smith, and she came out backward with a covered basket held against her chest in both arms, and she turned the corner past him with about eleven inches to spare and went off down the alley toward Fell Street without registering that he existed.

The door stood open behind her for a count of four.

What Wick saw in those four seconds, in the light of a room he never entered that night, was this.

A long bench. Lamps over it, good ones, four of them, which is more light than any workshop in the Loam would spend. A rack of small tools on a board, laid out with the spaces marked, in the way a person lays out tools when there are eleven of them and losing one matters.

Three pans on a cured-iron stand, over a slow heat, and the smell coming out of the doorway was the smell off the whole of Fell Street, sweetish and faintly like a rendering shed, except that at four feet it was not faint at all and Wick had to put his hand over his mouth.

A man sitting on a stool with his sleeve rolled to the shoulder and his arm laid out on the bench on a folded cloth, not moving, looking at the ceiling.

And a girl bent over the arm with a lamp brought down close and something small and bright in her right hand, working.

He could not see her face. He could see the back of a head with hair cut short and badly, and a shoulder, and a right hand doing something extremely fine, very slowly, with the whole of a person's attention behind it.

The door shut.

Wick stood in an alley in Seed with his hand still over his mouth.

He had come to Marrowfield with a shape in his head and had been carrying it for four months, and the shape was a girl in trouble or a girl ashamed or a girl who had got in with somebody, and every single one of those had a door he knew how to knock on.

The shape had not, at any point in four months, been *a girl who is extremely good at something.*

---

He went back to the room over the chandler's at half past seven and Nettle looked up from the ledger and said, "Well?"

"I didn't knock."

"Right."

Wick stopped in the doorway with his coat half off.

"That's mine," he said.

"I know it's yours. I'm using it." Nettle went back to the column. "You gave it me in Gather and I've been saving it, and if you want it back you'll have to knock on the door."

He hung the coat up.

"That's a low thing to do."

"It's an *effective* thing to do, and I'd not have done it if you'd come in here with a story." She turned the page round. "You came in and said four words and one of them was *didn't*. That's the best I've had off you since the Slack."

She tapped the ledger.

"Now come and look at this, because I've been at it since four and I think your seam's lying to you."

---

She had done a thing he would not have thought of, which he minded slightly and said so, and she took it as the compliment it was.

He plotted the seam's distance from the middle. That was the obvious thing and he had been doing it since Gather: fifteen, eleven and a quarter, ten and a half, nine and a half, a curve that flattened and flattened and looked as though it would never quite arrive.

Nettle had plotted something else.

"You've been measuring how far off it is," she said. "I've been measuring how *long* it is."

"You can't. You can't get in there."

"No. *You* have. Every night since Dark, because I've been making you." She ran a finger down a second column he had filled in himself, forty-one entries, and had never once looked at as a set. "You walk out along it and you count until you give up. You wrote it down every time because you write everything down. Look at it."

Wick looked at it.

**Dark, the fourth:** *walked it 900 paces one way before I gave up. Still going. Still a hand across.*
**Dark, the nineteenth:** *1,600. Gave up.*
**Deep, the second:** *2,400.*
**Deep, the twenty-first:** *4,100.*
**Still, the ninth:** *6,800.*
**Seed, the third:** *11,000. Legs went. Still a hand across.*

He sat down slowly on the end of the bed.

"That's not slowing," he said.

"No."

"That's — " He was doing it in his head, badly, twice. "It's doubling. It's about doubling every three weeks, and it's been doing it since Dark, and I've written every one of those numbers down myself and I have never once put them next to each other."

"Because you were looking at the middle," said Nettle. "Same as always. You look at where you are and not where it is."

Wick sat on the bed in a rented room over a chandler's with two columns of his own handwriting in front of him.

The first column said: *it is coming at you more and more slowly and it may never arrive.*

The second column said: *it is getting longer twice as fast every three weeks.*

Both of them were his measurements. Both of them were correct. He had made a shape out of the first one in Dark and had written *asymptotic* underneath it and had been quietly relieved for two months.

"It's not slowing down," he said. "It's *spreading out*."

"That's what I thought."

"It's not coming at the middle at all. It's — " and he stopped, because he did not have the next word, and would not have it for four months.

Nettle waited.

"I don't know," said Wick. "I've got two numbers and no idea. Write it under the ledger heading."

She wrote it under *things I don't understand yet*, which went to thirty-nine entries that night, in a hand considerably better than his.

> *39. Why is it in a hurry sideways and not in a hurry at me?*

# Chapter 3 — Sixty-One Fell Street

He went back on the twentieth and knocked, and it took him four seconds, and he had spent two days building up to it.

The big woman opened the door with her sleeves rolled and flour on one forearm, which he had not expected and which threw him entirely.

"We're shut."

"I'm not a customer."

"Then we're *very* shut." She looked at him properly, top to bottom, the way you look at a horse. "You're the one who sat on my crates for an hour on the eighteenth."

"...Yes."

"Mm," said the big woman, and stood aside.

---

The workshop was warmer than any room he had been in since Hollow Alder and it smelled so strongly that his eyes ran for the first minute and then stopped, permanently, and he never smelled it properly again for the rest of his life in that building.

Four lamps over a long bench. A rack of small tools with the spaces marked. Three pans on a cured-iron stand over a slow heat, and a fourth on the floor with a lid on it and a brick on the lid.

There was nobody else there.

"Sit," said the big woman, and pointed at a stool, and went and did something to one of the pans and looked at it for a count of about forty and then took it off the heat.

Then she sat down opposite him on the bench with her hands on her knees, and Wick understood, in the way you understand these things, that she was about eleven times more dangerous than she looked and that she looked fairly dangerous.

"Name."

"Wick Alder."

Her face did not change at all.

"Say the rest," she said.

"Of Hollow Alder in the Loam. I've been in Marrowfield since the Slack. I'm on the boundary at Hearth and I take Ember writs." He had rehearsed this bit. "I'm looking for my sister. Bri Alder. She came here in Y-eight-ten and she's sent money home three times and no word, and I've been asking in nine places since Dark and a Hall runner told me a small woman with the Loam in her voice takes parcels off him at this door."

The big woman was quiet.

"Twice a month," she said. "Since Deep."

"Yes."

"That's her. She's not here. She's at Tallow till the fourth."

---

Most of the rest of it came out of her in about a quarter of an hour, and she gave it to him plainly and without any softening whatsoever, and he came to understand later that this was the single greatest kindness anybody did him that year.

Her name was **Doreth Faring** and she had been a Grafter for twenty-two years under a Concord licence and she was the third best in Marrowfield, and she said all three of those things about herself in the flat voice a person uses to read out a distance.

Wick had heard those exact words before. He had heard them in a yard behind a Hall in Dust, out of a fifteen-year-old with a burn on her forearm, and it took him a full second and a half to make the two things touch, and when they touched he sat very still.

"You're Nettle's mother."

Doreth Faring's hands did not move on her knees.

"Say that again," she said.

"Annet. Annet Faring. Two feet across, seven and four, coherence true." Wick heard his own voice come out wrong. "She's been living in a room over a chandler's on Cross Street since the Slack. She's four hundred yards from this door and she's been four hundred yards from this door for four months."

The workshop was extremely quiet. One of the pans ticked.

"Four months," said Doreth Faring.

"Yes."

"And you didn't know I was her mother."

"I knew her mother was a Grafter. I knew twenty-two years and third best. I have known that since Dust." He put a hand over his eyes. "There's four Grafters in this town. I have walked past this building about nine times a week for four months. I have *asked at the front office*."

"Mm," said Doreth Faring, and got up, and went and put the pan back on the heat because her hands needed something to do, and stood with her back to him for a good while.

"She won't come," she said, to the wall.

"I don't think she knows how."

"No." Doreth turned round. "Nor do I, and I've had six years at it, and I'd thank you not to fix it for us."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were about to offer."

"...Yes," said Wick.

"Then don't." She came back and sat down. "You've had a shock and you'd like to be useful. That's decent of you and it's not what's wanted. What's wanted is that when you go back to that room tonight you tell her you were here, and you tell her the whole of what was said, and then you shut your mouth and let her do whatever she does with it, and if that takes four years then it takes four years."

Wick nodded.

"Now," said Doreth Faring. "Your sister."

---

"Bri Alder came in here in Rust of Y-eight-ten with a licence-application she'd never filed, thirteen feet of brick, and about nine shillings," she said. "She'd been in this town eleven weeks. She'd taken kitchen work and then carting work and then she'd got two months at the Hall as a runner and lost it, and I'd rather she told you why than me."

"All right."

"I took her on to sweep." Doreth's mouth moved. "That is not a euphemism. She swept this floor for seven months and I paid her three shilling a week and she never once asked me for anything else, and I have taken on eleven girls to sweep in twenty-two years and nine of them asked inside a fortnight."

"What changed?"

"She got good at the sweeping." Doreth said it entirely seriously. "You will not understand that and I'll not explain it. What I'll tell you is that in Green of eight-eleven I had a man on that bench with an arm that had gone bad round a soldier's graft that another Grafter put in nine years before, and I could not get the seat of it, and I had been at it two hours and I was going to have to take the arm, and your sister — who had swept my floor for seven months and had never touched a tool — said, from over by the door, *it's not in the arm, it's in the shoulder, look how he's sitting.*"

Wick did not say anything.

"And she was right," said Doreth Faring, "and I have thought about that afternoon every week since, and I do not know how she knew, and neither does she, and she has said four different things about it and I think all four were guesses."

---

"So she's a Grafter."

"No." Doreth held up one flat hand, and there was iron in it. "Get that straight now, because it is the whole of her situation and it is why she has not written to your grandmother.

"She is not a Grafter. She is not a journeyman and she is not an apprentice and she is not on the Concord register and she is not going to be, and that is not because she is not good enough. She is better at nine parts of this work than any journeyman I have had, and she is fifteen months from being better at it than me, and I do not say that."

"Then why isn't she on the register?"

"Because the Concord admits apprentices at *fourteen*," said Doreth Faring, "and takes indentures of seven years, and requires a bond of eleven pound, and she was seventeen and had nine shillings and no family in this town, and by the time she was any good she was eighteen, and there is no door in the Concord's rules that opens at eighteen. There is not one. I have looked at it for two years and I have written to Sennet twice and I have had two very polite replies."

She sat back.

"So she works in this room and she is paid out of my pocket and her name is on nothing. She may not cure a Seed under her own hand. She may not sign a fitting. Every graft she has done — and she has done a hundred and forty — is written in the book as **mine**, because if it were written as hers it would be an unlicensed fitting, and that is not a fine. That is the Concord, and the Concord takes the licence off the *supervisor*, and then there is no workshop and there is no wage and there is no her."

---

"Can I ask you something rude?" said Wick.

"You can ask."

"What's a graft actually *for*?"

Doreth Faring looked at him for a moment, and then did a thing he did not expect, which was to roll her own left sleeve up to the elbow.

There were four.

He knew what he was looking at because Nettle had shown him one in a yard in Dust — a shiny place a quarter of an inch across where something had gone in and stayed. Doreth had four, in a neat line up the inside of the forearm, spaced like buttons, old and well done and nearly invisible unless you were told.

"Chalk-hound, for the hands," she said, touching them in order. "River-drake, for the heat, because you cannot hold a pan at that temperature for two hours with an ordinary arm. Nine-in-a-coat, which cost me eleven pound and is the reason I can feel the seat of a fitting instead of guessing at it. And a tallow-crawler, which is worth four copper and which I put in myself when I was twenty because I was frightened of my own hands shaking."

She rolled the sleeve down.

"Nought point nine weight," said Doreth Faring. "Nine feet of daub. That's what I opened with, and that is what I have got now, and it has not moved in twenty-two years and it never will."

Wick did not say anything.

"So a graft is for this," she said. "It is for a woman with nine feet of daub doing work that a Wold couldn't do, and doing it for twenty-two years, and being paid for it. That's the whole of what it's for and there isn't a second thing."

"Nettle says it isn't yours."

"Nettle is *right*." Doreth said it without any heat at all, and that was somehow much worse. "She's right and she has been right since she was fourteen and I have never once argued with her about it. It isn't mine. I have four workings and not one of them came out of me. Every time I use my hands I am reading a letter somebody else wrote."

"Then why—"

"Because I had a choice between something that was mine and nothing, and *nothing is not a thing you can keep*." She put her hands flat on her knees. "That's the whole argument and I'd like you to notice it's not much of one. It isn't clever. It's just what it costs.

"And my daughter can afford to be right, because she's got seven and a half weight and she can hit a wall and make a mark, and there is not one day in her life she has needed a borrowed working to put bread on a table. And when she tells me it isn't hers, she is telling the truth, and she is also telling it from about eleven feet up in the air, and I have never once said so to her because she would take it as me asking."

She looked at the pan.

"Your sister could afford it for exactly as long as she had nine shillings," said Doreth Faring.

---

Wick sat on a stool in a warm room that smelled of cured Seed and did the arithmetic, because that is what he does.

A hundred and forty fittings. Two years and four months. Three parcels of eight shillings.

"She sends money home," he said, "and she doesn't write, because if she wrote she'd have to say what she does."

"She would have to say what she is," said Doreth Faring. "Which is a woman of nineteen who is very good at a trade she is not allowed to be in, and who has been doing it for two years in somebody else's name, and who will be doing it in somebody else's name when she is forty unless something changes, and nothing is going to change, because I have tried."

"Does she know I'm here?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell her?"

"Yes," said Doreth. "Tonight, in a letter to Tallow, because she is going to hear it from me and not walk in off a cart on the fourth and find you sat on my stool." She stood up, which ended the conversation. "And I'll tell you the last thing and then you'll go, because I've a pan on.

"Your sister has got thirteen feet of brick and coherence *settled* and about two and a half weight, and she is the second-best fitter in this town, and she cannot put her name to a single thing she does.

"And my daughter has got two feet of glass and seven and a half weight and the best coherence in this office, and she will not let me put a thing in her arm that would let her use it.

"And I have had six years of the pair of them," said Doreth Faring, "and I do not know which of them is right, and anybody who tells you they do is selling something."

---

At the corner of Fell Street he had to stop and lean on a wall.

Not because of Bri. Bri he could carry; Bri was bad and it was a bad he could look at.

It was the last thing she had said, and the way she had said it — flat, like reading out a distance — and the fact that a woman who had been at it twenty-two years and had a daughter in it did not know the answer.

Because Wick had had an answer since Dust. He had had it since a yard behind a Hall of Hunt, out of a girl on a water butt, and he had never once examined it, and it was: *a graft is not yours, so it doesn't count.*

He had believed that for four months, comfortably, without paying anything for it.

And a hundred and forty people in this town had an arm that worked because of a woman who was not allowed to have a name.

---

Nettle was at the table when he came in, with the ledger open and the second column laid out in her good hand, and she looked up and said, "Well?"

"I knocked."

"And?"

Wick shut the door and stood with his back against it, because he had worked out on the walk home that there was no version of this that was not a shock, and that the least bad thing he could do was the thing Doreth Faring had told him to do, which was to say the whole of it and then shut his mouth.

"Bri's not there," he said. "She's at Tallow till the fourth. She's been working in that workshop since Rust of eight-ten and she's done a hundred and forty fittings and every one of them is written down under somebody else's name, because she's nineteen and the Concord takes apprentices at fourteen and there's no door."

"Right," said Nettle. "So who's she working for?"

"Doreth Faring," said Wick.

Nettle did not move at all.

"Say that again," she said.


---

# Chapter 4 — Four Hundred Yards

*(Nettle)*

She let him say the whole of it, which took eleven minutes, and she did not interrupt once, and that was the most frightening thing she did all winter.

Wick had got that far in four months. Nettle had known him since Dust and could read him about as well as anybody ever had, and she watched him work through it in the order Doreth had told him to: Bri, the sweeping, the shoulder, the hundred and forty fittings in someone else's name. And she watched him *not* say the thing he wanted to say, which was *why haven't you been*, and she gave him full credit for it and did not tell him so.

At the end he stopped and put his hands flat on the table.

"That's all of it," he said. "She told me to say all of it and then shut up, so that's me shut up."

"Good," said Nettle.

Then she got up and put her coat on and went out.

---

She walked to the end of Cross Street and stood at the corner where it met Fell, and from that corner, on a clear night, you could see the light in the top window of sixty-one.

Four hundred yards. Six minutes at a walk. She had done the six minutes about nine thousand times in her life and had not done them since the Slack.

The light was on.

She stood at the corner for as long as it takes a person to be honest with themselves, which for Nettle was about ninety seconds, because she was extremely fast at it and had never once found it useful.

*You are not frightened of her.* True.
*You are not angry at her.* True, and had been true for about four years, which was the annoying part.
*You do not think she is wrong.* Also true. That one had gone at fifteen and had never come back.

She turned round and walked home.

---

Here is what happened, and she had never said it out loud to anybody and did not say it to Wick that night or for a long time afterward.

It was not a row. There had never been a row, and that was the whole of the problem, because a row you can go back and apologise for.

It was Thaw of Y-eight-twelve, three months before a boy in a village on thin ground put a candle on a gravel bar, and it was the fourth reassessment, and Nettle had walked home from the Cross Street office with a chit in her hand that said *result unchanged* on it for the fourth time.

Her mother had been at the bench. She had looked up and said, "Well?"

And Nettle had said "unchanged," and her mother's face had done something, and then Doreth Faring had put down what she was holding and had cried, standing up, with her hands at her sides, for about forty seconds, which she had done twice in Nettle's life.

And then she had stopped, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and said — kindly, and this is the part, this is the entire part — she had said:

"Annet. Love. Will you let me put one in."

And Nettle had said: "I'd rather be capped than be you."

---

She had meant it.

That was the thing she could not get round and had been failing to get round for eleven months. If she had not meant it, she could have gone back the next morning and said *I didn't mean that*, and Doreth would have taken it, because Doreth took everything.

But she had meant it, precisely, in the specific sense in which she had said it, which was: *I would rather have nothing that works than have a life made out of things that were somebody else's.*

And that was true, and it was the truest thing about her, and she still believed it at that corner at the end of Cross Street eleven months later.

And it was also, said out loud in that room to that woman with four buttons up her left forearm and twenty-two years behind her and a wet face, the single cruellest sentence Nettle had ever constructed, and she had constructed it in about a second and a half, because she was extremely fast at it.

She had moved her things out over the following fortnight. Nobody had asked her to. Doreth had helped her carry the chest.

And in eleven months neither of them had said one word about it, because Nettle could not apologise for a thing she still believed, and Doreth would not ask her to stop believing it, and so they had arrived at four hundred yards, and stayed there.

---

Wick was still up when she got in. He had the ledger out and had done nothing to it, which was how she knew he had been sitting there the whole hour.

"I didn't go," she said.

"I know."

"I got to the corner."

"That's four hundred yards further than the last four months."

"Don't," said Nettle, "manage me."

"I'm not managing you, I'm doing arithmetic." He did not look up. "It's what I've got instead of being any use."

She sat down.

They did not say anything for a bit, and the chandler's stove ticked, and down in the street somebody was having an argument about a horse.

"Your sister's better at it than my mother," said Nettle.

"That's not what she said."

"That's exactly what she said. She said fifteen months, and she said *I do not say that*, and if you'd heard her say a thing like that about anybody else you'd have understood it." Nettle pulled the ledger over and turned it round. "Doreth Faring has never once in her life overstated a number."

"All right."

"So there's a woman in this town who is going to be the best fitter in the Loam inside two years and she cannot sign her own work," said Nettle, "and there's another one who's the third best and has got four borrowed workings in her arm and a licence, and the difference between them isn't skill, it's that one of them was fourteen at the right time and the other one was seventeen."

"Yes."

"And I've got seven and a half weight and I'm not allowed to be anything either, and *my* reason is that a brass hoop says my ground's two feet across." She was going faster now. "Three of us. Three, out of about six people I've ever met properly, and all three of us are stuck behind a *rule with a number in it*, and every single one of those numbers was written down by somebody who never once imagined us."

Wick had gone very still.

"Say that again," he said.

"Don't do that. That's mine, I said it first."

"I'm not — " He put his hand flat on the table. "Nettle. Say the last bit again."

"Every one of those numbers was written down by somebody who never imagined us."

Wick sat back.

"The Concord admits at fourteen," he said slowly, "because that's when a Hold opens and that's when you've got most of your life left to indenture. It's not a *rule*, it's a — it's an assumption about when people start."

"So?"

"So it's the form having two boxes for feet." His voice had changed. "Camber crossed out FEET and wrote ACRES and then wrote a note saying the defect was in the schedule and not in the subject, and a man in an inked coat read that note eleven months ago and came four days' ride to sit on a bridge post because of it."

Nettle looked at him.

"You're not going to fix my mother," she said.

"No."

"Or Bri. Not with a form."

"No." Wick got the ledger and turned to the back, where he kept the list he had started in Long and had never stopped keeping, and wrote a line at the bottom of it in his awful hand. "But I've spent four months asking people in this town what my sister *does*, and not one of them could tell me, and the reason is that there is no word for it and no box for it and no line in any register anywhere.

"And I know exactly one person in the entire world whose whole job is finding those."

---

---

She did the thing at about two in the morning, when Wick had gone to sleep on the floor by the stove the way he did when he had been thinking too hard to get into a bed.

She had not gone in properly in eleven months. That was not a secret and it was not a decision; there had simply been nothing to go in *for*. A person walks their Hold to raise coherence, and hers was true, and had been true since she was fourteen, and there is no grade above true.

So she went in, at two in the morning, in a rented room over a chandler's, for the first time since Thaw.

Two feet of glass.

Flat as a plate. A dead circle. Struck sand gone smooth, and when she put her palm on it, it was neither warm nor cold, the way a thing is when it has never been anything else.

She stood in the middle of it, which took no walking at all, and looked at the edge.

*Like the world stops*, she had said to him, on a kerb on Cross Street in Dust, and he had asked whether it stopped like a wall or like the world stopping, and nobody in six years had ever asked her that.

She went and looked at it properly.

It did not stop like a wall. There was no ridge, no lip, no rim, no crust, no line of set anything. The glass went out to arm's length and then there was no glass, and there was no *not*-glass either; there was nothing at all, in the way there is nothing at all past the end of a thought.

Nettle stood at the edge of her entire country with her hands at her sides and worked it out in about four seconds, because she was extremely fast at it and had never once found it useful.

Wick had spent twenty-one nights walking a line in the dark to make a boundary, and had been graded on it, and had a rank because of it.

She had no boundary.

Not a bad one. Not a small one. **None.** There was nothing there to walk. There was nothing there to load. Camber's brass clamp had a place to push on a nine-foot room and a place to push on nine paces of warm damp and there was, in the whole of Annet Faring, nowhere to put it.

*Coherence: true.*

She said a word out loud that she would not have said in front of her mother.

Because that was not a grade. That was not an achievement and it had never been one and four registrars had written it on four chits and she had carried it round her neck for six years like a thing she had earned. Coherence is how well the terrain in a Hold fits together. Hers fit perfectly.

There was one thing in it and nothing to argue with.

She had been given full marks for an empty examination and had spent six years being quietly proud of it.

---

She came out and lay on her back in the dark with her eyes open and did not sleep, and at about four she got up and got the ledger, quietly, and turned to the heading of her own that she had been keeping since Dark, and wrote the fifth thing on it.

Then, because she was honest and because it was four in the morning, she went back and wrote a sixth underneath.

> *5. What is two feet of ground with no edge FOR?*
> *6. Ask him how he made one. Out loud. Using words. Tomorrow.*

She looked at the second one for some time.

Then she crossed out *tomorrow* and wrote *soon*, and then crossed out *soon* and wrote *tomorrow* again, and put the charcoal down and went to bed.

---

She read it upside down, because she read everything upside down and faster than he wrote it.

> *40. Who decides what the boxes are?*

"That's not a thing you don't understand," said Nettle. "That's a thing you've decided to be annoyed about."

"It's the same list."

"It is *not* the same list, and if you start putting that sort of thing on it the list stops being any use." She pulled it toward her, got the charcoal off him, crossed out the *40*, and wrote it out again lower down under a heading of her own.

Wick leaned over to see what she had called it.

**THINGS THAT ARE NOBODY'S FAULT AND SOMEBODY'S JOB.**

"That's a terrible heading," he said.

"It's a *long* heading. It's not a terrible one." Nettle put the charcoal down. "And there's four things on it already and I've been keeping it since Dark, and I'd thank you not to look at the other three."


---

# Chapter 5 — The Register of Workings

It cost two shillings to search the Register and four to enter something in it, and Wick had been putting it off since the Slack for the same reason everybody puts it off, which is that four shillings is four shillings and a working does not stop working because it has no name.

Nettle made him go on the twenty-third.

"You've got two unregistered workings and a mark on your tally," she said, over breakfast, in the tone she used when a thing had already been decided somewhere she had not invited him. "Every clerk in that office has *pending examination* written against your name, and pending is the worst word in that building, and you have been pending since Rust."

"It's four shillings."

"It's four shillings and you've got four pound two put by and you have not spent one penny of it since the Slack because you're frightened of it," said Nettle. "Also I want to see this."

---

The Register did not live in the main office. It lived in a room at the back of the Ledger building on Cross Street, up two steps and down four, with a counter across it and, behind the counter, shelving that went up to a ceiling Wick could not see the top of.

There were, the clerk told him without being asked, four hundred and eleven thousand entries.

"Since when?"

"Since always." The clerk was about sixty and had the specific serenity of somebody who has found the correct job. "Y-one-nineteen is the earliest, and it is a working for keeping bees warm, and it is still on the shelf, and I have read it."

"Does anyone use it?"

"No," said the clerk, with enormous satisfaction. "Nobody has used it in six hundred years. That is not the point of the Register."

---

The form was called a **Schedule of Working (First Entry)** and it had eleven parts and they went through it at the counter and it took the whole of the afternoon.

*Part one: the subject.* Name, tally, rank. The clerk wrote *Hearth (on the boundary)* without any change of expression whatsoever and Wick was so grateful for that he nearly said something.

*Part two: the terrain of origin.*

"Warm damp," said Wick. "Nine and a half paces, at the middle."

"Source?"

"Tallow-crawler. Five of them, planted Long through High of last year."

"Good." The clerk wrote. "That is a clean one. Nine entries in ten are a mess at part two because people cannot remember where a thing came from. Next."

*Part three: the effect.*

"I put warm wet ground into what I'm touching."

"How much?"

"Depends what I've got. On a full night it's most of a pan of water, spread over about a dinner plate."

"Duration?"

"It stays. It's ground, it doesn't go anywhere. It dries the way anything dries."

*Part four: cost.* Wick gave him the breath figures and the eight-day recovery from the barrows, and the clerk stopped writing and looked at him.

"You have got a *number* for your recovery."

"Sixty-one breaths a night and eight days from empty, and it comes back all at once at the end and not evenly, and I don't know why."

"Nobody knows why," said the clerk, "and about four people in four hundred and eleven thousand have written it down. Carry on."

---

They got stuck at part six.

*Part six: classification.*

There were, printed on the form, five boxes, and they had been printed on that form since Y-four-forty.

> ☐ *Body* — the working acts upon the practitioner's own person.
> ☐ *Export* — the working acts upon a thing the practitioner touches.
> ☐ *Verge* — the working acts within the practitioner's imposed ground.
> ☐ *Construct* — the working produces a thing which persists apart from the practitioner.
> ☐ *Ward* — the working is fixed upon real ground.

"The first one's Export," said Wick. "That's easy. I touch a thing and warm wet goes into it."

"Agreed. Export." The clerk ticked it. "The second."

"The second is I put warm wet into a thing and then I take the water back out, and what's left sets hard."

The clerk's pen went down and did not come up for a while.

"Which box," he said.

"Export?"

"You said *and then*." The clerk turned the form round. "An export is one act. You are describing two acts. The second undoes the first, and what is left over is a third thing that neither of them made. Is the salt an export?"

"No. The salt's already there. It's in the ground. My whole Hold is salt."

"So the working exports *water*, and then removes water, and the result is a property of a terrain you did not act on at all."

"...Yes."

"Then it is not an Export." The clerk sat back. "It is not a Body, because it is not on your person — you told me you have done it to a chalk-hound. It is not a Verge, because you are Hearth and your Verge stops at your skin. It is not a Construct, because the crust is not a thing you made, it is *your own ground behaving*. And it is not a Ward, because it is not fixed to real ground; it goes wherever your hand goes."

Nettle, from the bench by the door, said: "So there's no box."

"There is no box," said the clerk.

---

Here is what Wick did not expect, and what he thought about for a long time afterward.

The clerk was *delighted*.

He got down off his stool. He went and got a second stool and put it behind the counter and sat on it instead. He sent a boy out for tea. He turned the form over and started writing on the back of it in a hand that got faster and worse, and he said, three separate times, "Now, wait," to a fifteen-year-old who had not been going to interrupt.

"Where does it come from," he said at last. "Not the terrain. Not the *effect*. What is the actual seat of it."

"The border," said Wick.

"Say the whole of it."

"My warm damp's got a border with the salt. Nine and a half paces across, twenty-eight round. I walked it for twenty-one nights and the salt at the edge took up the water out of the wet ground and set hard, and that's a rim, and the rim's what got me graded in Rust." He had never said this to a stranger and found it came out fast. "And the working isn't the damp and it isn't the salt. It's the *thing that happens where they meet*. I can only do it because I've got both of them and they touch."

The clerk stopped writing.

"There is a word for that," he said, "and it is not on this form, and I have been in this room for thirty-four years and I have entered perhaps eleven of them and every one of them was a Grafter."

"What's the word?"

"**Confluence**," said the clerk. "Where two terrains border, the border does a thing that neither of them does. The Concord has known about it since Y-six-hundred because they cure Seeds in pairs and the pans do things the pans should not do, and it is in *their* schedule and it has been in their schedule for two hundred years, and it has never once been added to ours, because a practitioner has one Hold and one Hold is a room and a room does not have interior borders worth the name."

He looked up.

"Everybody has four or five terrains in nine feet," he said. "They are all touching. Nobody has ever been able to *walk* the line between two of them, because in a nine-foot room the line between two terrains is about a foot long and it is under the fire."

---

They finished at ten past six and the entry ran to three sheets, which the clerk said was the longest first entry he had taken since Y-eight-oh-four.

> **REGISTER OF WORKINGS — ENTRY 411,206**
> Subject: Wick Alder, of Hollow Alder, the Loam. Hearth (on the boundary).
> **1. The Alder Standing Working.** *Common name: the wet.* Class: Export.
> Terrain of origin: warm damp. Effect: transfer of warm saturated ground to a touched
> surface. Cost: variable to total. Recovery from total: eight days, non-linear.
> **2. The Alder Setting Working.** *Common name: the crust.* Class: **Confluence**
> (entered under protest of the form; see attached memorandum).
> Seat: the border of *warm damp* and *salt flat*, twenty-eight paces, walked.
> Effect: transfer and withdrawal of water leaving a set salt shell, ~2mm, on a touched
> surface. Cost: total. Recovery: as above.
> Entered by: H. Sarrow, Registrar of Workings, Marrowfield.

Wick read his own name on it four times.

"Sarrow," he said.

"Mm."

"Like the line. The Sarrow's line. The candle and the string."

"My great-grandfather," said the clerk, without looking up, "and he was a nuisance, and it is in the field manual at section four, and I have spent thirty-four years being asked about it." He blotted the sheet. "Four shillings."

Wick paid four shillings.

---

There was a thing he had not thought about, and Sarrow raised it while he was blotting the second sheet, in the mild voice of a man mentioning the weather.

"You understand this is public."

"...Public how?"

"Two shillings and anybody may search the Register." Sarrow squared the sheets. "That is the whole of what it is for. A working that nobody may look up is a rumour, and the Sill ran on rumour for a hundred and nineteen years and it was a disaster and I will tell you about it if you have four hours."

"So anybody can read what I can do."

"Anybody can read what you have *told* us you can do, which is not the same and which is why I asked you eleven questions about the cost." He put the sheets in a wire basket. "And they will. Yours will be searched. Everything new is searched; there are men in this town who come in on the first of the month and read the last thirty entries the way other men read a racing sheet."

"How many people have searched my name?"

Sarrow looked up.

"That is a very good question and it is the second thing nobody asks," he said. "Searches are logged. Yours has been searched four times since Rust."

Wick stood at the counter.

"I've not had a working in it since Rust," he said. "There's been nothing to find."

"There has been your *tally*," said Sarrow. "Acreage forty-three point nine, weight nought point six, Hearth held on the boundary, coherence ragged second class, settled nought point nought nought one four. Four people have paid two shillings each to read that."

"Who?"

"I am not permitted to tell you and I would not if I were." He said it without apology. "You may know that a thing was looked at. You may not know who looked. That has been the rule since Y-four-eleven and it is the only reason anybody files anything true."

---

Pell was on the bottom step, sitting down, with his satchel on his knees and his coat done up wrong.

"Deputy Under-Auditor Emeritus of the Third Circuit, Provisional, Seconded, and Attached," he said, before either of them had got a word out. "It has got longer. I am not going to explain the last one."

"You've been sat there the whole time."

"Two hours and eleven minutes. I did not come in, because a first entry takes as long as it takes and a man in an inked coat in the doorway makes clerks careful." Pell got up, joint by joint. "Sarrow gave you *Confluence*."

"Under protest of the form."

"Under protest of the form," Pell repeated, with relish. "That is eleven words that will be read in Sennet-on-Weft inside a month, because Harl Sarrow has entered four hundred and eleven thousand workings and has protested a form nine times in thirty-four years, and every single one of the nine went to the Quorum."

Nettle said: "Is that bad?"

"It is not bad. It is *loud*," said Ossian Pell, and put his hat on. "And I would like the boy to notice that he has spent four shillings and made a noise that carries three hundred miles, and that he did it by answering eleven questions honestly at a counter, which is how nearly everything of consequence has ever been done in this country."

---

Outside, on the steps, in the dark, Nettle said: "*Confluence.*"

"I know."

"Say the rest, you've been holding it since the counter."

Wick stood on the steps of the Ledger office on Cross Street with a receipt in his hand.

"I've got a salt flat and a warm damp and six chalk pegs," he said. "That's eight bits of ground. And the salt touches every single one of them, because the salt's *everything*, and every one of those borders is a different pair."

"That's seven borders."

"That's seven borders and I have walked exactly one of them," said Wick, "and it took me twenty-one nights and it got me a rank."

Nettle went down two steps and turned round so she was looking up at him, which she did when she wanted his full attention and had learned worked.

"How long are the other six?" she said.

And Wick, who had paced the distance out to peg one four hundred times, who had a survey line and a column and a curve and four pages of a feed ledger in the worst handwriting in the Loam, opened his mouth to give her the number.

And found that he did not have it.

He had measured the distance *to* each peg. He had never once measured the way *round* one.
